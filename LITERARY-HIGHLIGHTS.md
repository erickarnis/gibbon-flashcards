# Gibbon — Literary highlights from Volumes I–II

Thirty-two passages for rereading, chosen for their imagery, rhythm, irony, and moral force. Wording follows the supplied Mueller edition; every passage now appears on a linked card that develops its historical setting. This is a curated selection, not a second deck.

## A citizen borrows a triumph

The reliefs on Trajan’s Column turn distant victories into a shared memory.

> The veteran soldier contemplated the story of his own campaigns, and by an easy illusion of national vanity, the peaceful citizen associated himself to the honors of the triumph.

[Gibbon, Chapter II, L637](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L637).

**Card:** [6. Trajan — a history in marble](01-opening-emperors.md#gibbon-01-trajan-column)

## The applause given to destroyers

Trajan’s emulation of Alexander opens into a judgment on military glory.

> as long as mankind shall continue to bestow more liberal applause on their destroyers than on their benefactors, the thirst of military glory will ever be the vice of the most exalted characters.

[Gibbon, Chapter I, L568](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L568).

**Card:** [8. Trajan — the dangerous model](01-opening-emperors.md#gibbon-01-trajan-alexander)

## Three spectators of religion

Gibbon sets the people, the philosopher, and the magistrate side by side.

> The various modes of worship which prevailed in the Roman world were all considered by the people as equally true; by the philosopher, as equally false; and by the magistrate, as equally useful.

[Gibbon, Chapter II, L601](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L601).

**Card:** [35. Religion — three spectators](01-opening-emperors.md#gibbon-01-theme-religion)

## The happy difficulty of writing history

The peaceful reign of Antoninus Pius offers the historian unusually little to tell.

> His reign is marked by the rare advantage of furnishing very few materials for history; which is, indeed, little more than the register of the crimes, follies, and misfortunes of mankind.

[Gibbon, Chapter III, L724](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L724).

**Card:** [19. Antoninus Pius — the happy difficulty for historians](01-opening-emperors.md#gibbon-01-pius-history)

## A safe and dreary prison

The extent of imperial power becomes the impossibility of escape.

> But the empire of the Romans filled the world, and when the empire fell into the hands of a single person, the world became a safe and dreary prison for his enemies.

[Gibbon, Chapter III, L740](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L740).

**Card:** [18. An empire without an outside](01b-empire-and-constitution.md#gibbon-01b-world-prison)

## Education and disposition

Marcus’s efforts to educate Commodus provoke one of Gibbon’s bleakest epigrams.

> But the power of instruction is seldom of much efficacy, except in those happy dispositions where it is almost superfluous.

[Gibbon, Chapter IV, L759](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L759).

**Card:** [28. Marcus Aurelius — preparing Commodus](01-opening-emperors.md#gibbon-01-marcus-commodus)

## The danger nearest home

The murder of Commodus exposes the limits of terror as a means of government.

> He had shed with impunity the noblest blood of Rome: he perished as soon as he was dreaded by his own domestics.

[Gibbon, Chapter IV, L785](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L785).

**Card:** [9. Commodus — the danger nearest home](02-commodus-to-gallienus.md#gibbon-02-commodus-death)

## The ascent and the summit

Severus gains the empire and discovers the disappointment of possessing it.

> The ascent to greatness, however steep and dangerous, may entertain an active spirit with the consciousness and exercise of its own powers: but the possession of a throne could never yet afford a lasting satisfaction to an ambitious mind.

[Gibbon, Chapter VI, L883](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L883).

**Card:** [21. Severus — the disappointment of greatness](02-commodus-to-gallienus.md#gibbon-02-severus-satisfaction)

## The fetters of possession

Gibbon contrasts the independence he attributes to the Germans with the dependence of richer subjects.

> Their poverty secured their freedom, since our desires and our possessions are the strongest fetters of despotism.

[Gibbon, Chapter IX, L1114](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1114).

**Card:** [48. Germany — the fetters of possession](02-commodus-to-gallienus.md#gibbon-02-germany-poverty)

## The immortality of song

Warriors seek in remembered glory what priests promise beyond the grave.

> The immortality so vainly promised by the priests was in some degree conferred by the bards.

[Gibbon, Chapter IX, L1134](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1134).

**Card:** [50. Germany — the immortality of song](02-commodus-to-gallienus.md#gibbon-02-germany-bards)

## From the cottage to the grave

The rapid elevation and destruction of third-century emperors cannot be separated from their subjects’ suffering.

> The rapid and perpetual transitions from the cottage to the throne, and from the throne to the grave, might have amused an indifferent philosopher; were it possible for a philosopher to remain indifferent amidst the general calamities of humankind.

[Gibbon, Chapter X, L1231](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1231).

**Card:** [65. Usurpation — the cost paid by the provinces](02-commodus-to-gallienus.md#gibbon-02-usurpers-cost)

## The melancholy walls

Aurelian’s walls protect Rome while admitting its vulnerability.

> It was a great but a melancholy labor, since the defense of the capital betrayed the decline of the monarchy.

[Gibbon, Chapter XI, L1276](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1276).

**Card:** [5. Aurelian — the melancholy walls](03-restoration-and-christianity.md#gibbon-03-aurelian-walls)

## The Senate’s last blaze

The senatorial revival under Tacitus, as Gibbon portrays it, lasts only a moment.

> The expiring senate displayed a sudden luster, blazed for a moment and was extinguished forever.

[Gibbon, Chapter XII, L1339](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1339).

**Card:** [13. Tacitus — the Senate’s last blaze](03-restoration-and-christianity.md#gibbon-03-tacitus-senate)

## Cabbages and power

The retired Diocletian answers Maximian’s invitation to return to the throne.

> He rejected the temptation with a smile of pity, calmly observing that if he could show Maximian the cabbages which he had planted with his own hands at Salona, he should no longer be urged to relinquish the enjoyment of happiness for the pursuit of power.

[Gibbon, Chapter XIII, L1452](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1452).

**Card:** [34. Diocletian — cabbages instead of empire](03-restoration-and-christianity.md#gibbon-03-cabbages)

## Heaven and earth

The historian and the theologian receive different tasks.

> The theologian may indulge the pleasing task of describing Religion as she descended from Heaven, arrayed in her native purity. A more melancholy duty is imposed on the historian. He must discover the inevitable mixture of error and corruption which she contracted in a long residence upon earth, among a weak and degenerate race of beings.

[Gibbon, Chapter XV, L1556](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1556).

**Card:** [46. Religion — the historian’s task](03-restoration-and-christianity.md#gibbon-03-historian-theologian)

## Before transcribing, believe

Gibbon pauses before the accumulated accounts of martyrdom.

> But I cannot determine what I ought to transcribe till I am satisfied how much I ought to believe.

[Gibbon, Chapter XVI, L1763](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1763).

**Card:** [68. Gibbon — what should history believe?](03-restoration-and-christianity.md#gibbon-03-evidence)

## Government as theatre

The court’s elaborate ranks prompt a comparison between authority and performance.

> By a philosophic observer, the system of the Roman government might have been mistaken for a splendid theater, filled with players of every character and degree, who repeated the language and imitated the passions of their original model.

[Gibbon, Chapter XVII, L1813](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1813).

**Card:** [5. Government as theatre](04-constantine-to-adrianople.md#gibbon-04-court-theatre)

## Who will ever be innocent?

Julian answers Delphidius, a prosecutor frustrated by a defendant’s denial.

> “Who will ever be found guilty,” exclaimed the vehement Delphidius, “if it be enough to deny?” “And who,” replied Julian, “will ever be innocent, if it be sufficient to affirm?”

[Gibbon, Chapter XIX, L1973](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1973).

**Card:** [24. Who will ever be innocent?](04-constantine-to-adrianople.md#gibbon-04-julian-evidence)

## Heaven decided in a cabinet

Theological controversy turns on the decisions of emperors.

> But as those princes presumed to extend their despotism over the faith, as well as over the lives and fortunes, of their subjects, the weight of their suffrage sometimes inclined the ecclesiastical balance: and the prerogatives of the King of Heaven were settled, or changed, or modified in the cabinet of an earthly monarch.

[Gibbon, Chapter XXI, L2097](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2097).

**Card:** [38. Heaven decided at court](04-constantine-to-adrianople.md#gibbon-04-creeds-power)

## Enthusiasm and imposture

Gibbon introduces his criticism of the magical practices associated with Julian’s philosophers.

> In every age, the absence of genuine inspiration is supplied by the strong illusions of enthusiasm and the mimic arts of imposture.

[Gibbon, Chapter XXIII, L2193](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2193).

**Card:** [49. Julian’s philosophers — enthusiasm and imposture](04-constantine-to-adrianople.md#gibbon-04-julian-theurgy)

## Two resting places for Julian

Philosopher and soldier each claim the dead emperor for a different company.

> The philosopher expressed a very reasonable wish that the disciple of Plato might have reposed amidst the groves of the academy; while the soldier exclaimed, in bolder accents, that the ashes of Julian should have been mingled with those of Caesar, in the field of Mars, and among the ancient monuments of Roman virtue.

[Gibbon, Chapter XXIV, L2272](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2272).

**Card:** [60. Julian — two imagined resting places](04-constantine-to-adrianople.md#gibbon-04-julian-two-graves)

## The ruler who will not judge

Provincial corruption makes imperial inaction a form of complicity.

> The prince who refuses to be the judge instructs the people to consider him as the accomplice of his ministers.

[Gibbon, Chapter XXV, L2335](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2335).

**Card:** [72. Theodosius — service and reward](04-constantine-to-adrianople.md#gibbon-04-elder-theodosius)

## The elements and ourselves

An earthquake introduces a history of human catastrophe.

> Without presuming to discuss the truth or propriety of these lofty speculations, the historian may content himself with an observation, which seems to be justified by experience, that man has much more to fear from the passions of his fellow-creatures than from the convulsions of the elements.

[Gibbon, Chapter XXVI, L2382](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2382).

**Card:** [75. A sinking empire and a sinking world](04-constantine-to-adrianople.md#gibbon-04-earthquake)

## The fish of the Danube

Gibbon answers the Christian writer Jerome’s claim that devastation extended even to the fish: the joke challenges exaggerated testimony.

> The various tribes that people the air, or the waters, are still less connected with the fate of the human species; and it is highly probable that the fish of the Danube would have felt more terror and distress from the approach of a voracious pike than from the hostile inroad of a Gothic army.

[Gibbon, Chapter XXVI, L2408](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2408).

**Card:** [80. The fish and the Gothic army](04-constantine-to-adrianople.md#gibbon-04-fish-danube)

## A doctrine he declines to learn

After the massacre of Gothic youths in Asia, Gibbon distinguishes expediency from humanity.

> The urgent consideration of the public safety may undoubtedly authorize the violation of every positive law. How far that, or any other, consideration may operate to dissolve the natural obligations of humanity and justice is a doctrine of which I still desire to remain ignorant.

[Gibbon, Chapter XXVI, L2410](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2410).

**Card:** [81. The limits of public safety](04-constantine-to-adrianople.md#gibbon-04-gothic-massacre)

## The happiest age

The opening prosperity gives the history its height from which to descend.

> If a man were called to fix the period in the history of the world during which the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous, he would, without hesitation, name that which elapsed from the death of Domitian to the accession of Commodus.

[Gibbon, Chapter III, L728](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L728).

**Card:** [31. Why prosperity comes first](01-opening-emperors.md#gibbon-01-theme-baseline)

## Virtue is not restraint

Good emperors cannot supply the constitutional protection that their successors will lack.

> The ideal restraints of the senate and the laws might serve to display the virtues, but could never correct the vices, of the emperor.

[Gibbon, Chapter III, L730](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L730).

**Card:** [33. Virtue is not a constitution](01-opening-emperors.md#gibbon-01-theme-fragility)

## Governed by names

Augustus preserves the language of liberty while securing personal rule.

> Augustus was sensible that mankind is governed by names; nor was he deceived in his expectation that the senate and people would submit to slavery, provided they were respectfully assured that they still enjoyed their ancient freedom.

[Gibbon, Chapter III, L704](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L704).

**Card:** [32. The constitution behind the names](01-opening-emperors.md#gibbon-01-theme-constitution)

## The sword and the sceptre

The Senate has surrendered the means of maintaining sovereignty.

> They soon experienced that those who refuse the sword must renounce the scepter.

[Gibbon, Chapter XII, L1349](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1349).

**Card:** [15. Probus — the sword and the sceptre](03-restoration-and-christianity.md#gibbon-03-probus-sword)

## The foundation beneath the army

The maxim attributed to the Persian king Ardashir I, whom Gibbon calls Artaxerxes, leads power back to justice.

> “The authority of the prince,” said Artaxerxes, “must be defended by a military force; that force can only be maintained by taxes; all taxes must, at last, fall upon agriculture; and agriculture can never flourish except under the protection of justice and moderation.”

[Gibbon, Chapter VIII, L1091](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1091).

**Card:** [47. Ardashir — the foundation beneath the army](02-commodus-to-gallienus.md#gibbon-02-ardashir-justice)

## The poison of peace

Gibbon locates a source of political weakness within the empire’s prosperity.

> This long peace, and the uniform government of the Romans, introduced a slow and secret poison into the vitals of the empire.

[Gibbon, Chapter II, L649](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L649).

**Card:** [36. The weakness concealed by peace](01-opening-emperors.md#gibbon-01-theme-civic-decline)


## The purpose of government

Gibbon’s praise of Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius makes public happiness the measure of a reign.

> Their united reigns are possibly the only period of history in which the happiness of a great people was the sole object of government.

[Gibbon, Chapter III, L722](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L722).

**Card:** [19. Antoninus Pius — the happy difficulty for historians](01-opening-emperors.md#gibbon-01-pius-history)
