# Gibbon — Constantine to Adrianople and the Gothic Settlement

Chapters XVII–XXVI, completing Volume II. All dates are AD.

## Constantinople and the machinery of empire — XVII

### 1. Constantinople — the new centre

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-capital -->
<a id="gibbon-04-capital"></a>

**Q:** Where did Constantine build his new capital, and why was the site so strong?

**A:** **Constantinople**, on the site of **Byzantium beside the Bosporus**, between Europe and Asia. Its defensible position and access to trade also placed the emperor nearer the Danube and Persian frontiers.

**Gibbon:** “The prospect of beauty, of safety, and of wealth, united in a single spot, was sufficient to justify the choice of Constantine.”

**More:** Work followed Constantine’s victory in **324**; the city was dedicated in **330**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1795](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1795); [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1797](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1797); [DIR: Constantine, Constantinople](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/conniei.htm).

### 2. The invisible guide

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-invisible-guide -->
<a id="gibbon-04-invisible-guide"></a>

**Q:** In the foundation legend, why would Constantine not stop enlarging his new city?

**A:** He claimed to follow an **invisible divine guide**. When attendants protested that the circuit already exceeded a great city, he would advance until that guide stopped.

**Gibbon:** “I shall still advance,” replied Constantine, “till HE, the invisible guide who marches before me, thinks proper to stop.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1797](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1797).

### 3. Statues without souls

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-borrowed-statues -->
<a id="gibbon-04-borrowed-statues"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon turn the decoration of Constantinople into a judgment on cultural decline?

**A:** Constantine could **transfer the masterpieces of Greece**, but could not recreate the genius that had made them. Imperial power could collect sculpture; it could not produce another great sculptor, poet, or orator.

**Gibbon:** “To revive the genius of Phidias and Lysippus surpassed indeed the power of a Roman emperor; but the immortal productions which they had bequeathed to posterity were exposed without defense to the rapacious vanity of a despot.”

**More:** **Phidias and Lysippus**, named in the quotation, were celebrated Greek sculptors.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1799](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1799).

### 4. The invitations of a master

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-capital-growth -->
<a id="gibbon-04-capital-growth"></a>

**Q:** How did Constantine attract inhabitants to his new capital?

**A:** He offered **palaces, lands, pensions, and privileges** to wealthy settlers. The court then attracted officials, traders, artisans, and servants; Egyptian grain supported the growing population.

**Gibbon:** “The invitations of a master are scarcely to be distinguished from commands; and the liberality of the emperor obtained a ready and cheerful obedience.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1807](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1807); [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1809](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1809).

### 5. Government as theatre

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-court-theatre -->
<a id="gibbon-04-court-theatre"></a>

**Q:** What does Gibbon see in the elaborate ranks and ceremonies of the later imperial court?

**A:** **Personal merit gives way to graded dependence**: each official displays a prescribed rank and imitates the majesty above him.

**Gibbon:** “By a philosophic observer, the system of the Roman government might have been mistaken for a splendid theater, filled with players of every character and degree, who repeated the language and imitated the passions of their original model.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1813](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1813).

### 6. The empty chair of Cicero

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-consuls -->
<a id="gibbon-04-consuls"></a>

**Q:** What remained of the consulship under Constantine and his successors?

**A:** **Prestige, ceremony, and the naming of the year**, with little independent power. Appointment came from the emperor; the title outlived the republican office.

**Gibbon:** “As soon as the consuls had discharged these customary duties, they were at liberty to retire into the shade of private life and to enjoy, during the remainder of the year, the undisturbed contemplation of their own greatness.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1817](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1817); [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1819](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1819).

### 7. Divide the powers

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-civil-military -->
<a id="gibbon-04-civil-military"></a>

**Q:** Why did Constantine remove military command from the praetorian prefects?

**A:** To make **rebellion harder**. Prefects retained civil and financial authority while other officers commanded the troops. A potential rival would no longer hold revenue, justice, and an army together.

**Gibbon:** “To secure his throne and the public tranquility from these formidable servants, Constantine resolved to divide the military from the civil administration and to establish, as a permanent and professional distinction, a practice which had been adopted only as an occasional expedient.”

**More:** This developed an earlier separation of administrative duties under Diocletian.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1823](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1823); [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1835](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1835); [Gibbon, Ch. XIII, L1444](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1444); [DIR: Constantine, Government](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/conniei.htm).

### 8. The court and the frontier

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-field-frontier -->
<a id="gibbon-04-field-frontier"></a>

**Q:** What danger does Gibbon see in the privileged troops of the court and the neglected frontier forces?

**A:** **Unequal rewards weaken the men facing the greatest danger**. He blames the move towards favoured troops in the interior for undermining frontier discipline and defence.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1837](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1837).

### 9. The eyes of the monarch

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-imperial-spies -->
<a id="gibbon-04-imperial-spies"></a>

**Q:** How did imperial messengers become instruments of fear in Gibbon’s account?

**A:** Their work expanded from carrying official news to **reporting on officials and private citizens**. Rewards for denunciation made surveillance a means of extortion.

**Gibbon:** “Their careless or criminal violation of truth and justice was covered by the consecrated mask of zeal; and they might securely aim their poisoned arrows at the breast either of the guilty or the innocent who had provoked their resentment or refused to purchase their silence”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1847](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1847).

### 10. The weight of taxation

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-taxes -->
<a id="gibbon-04-taxes"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon say taxation mattered more than executions to most imperial subjects?

**A:** Political cruelty chiefly threatened conspicuous individuals; **taxation reached the obscure millions**, pressing hardest on those with least to spare.

**Gibbon:** “The obscure millions of a great empire have much less to dread from the cruelty than from the avarice of their masters, and their humble happiness is principally affected by the grievance of excessive taxes, which, gently pressing on the wealthy, descend with accelerated weight on the meaner and more indigent classes of society.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVII, L1851](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1851).

## Constantine’s family and the succession — XVIII

### 11. Constantine — the reversed Augustus

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-constantine-character -->
<a id="gibbon-04-constantine-character"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon contrast the course of Constantine’s character with that of Augustus?

**A:** **Augustus improves after victory; Constantine deteriorates.** The successful hero becomes, in Gibbon’s judgment, a suspicious and lavish monarch whose magnificence is paid for by his subjects.

**Gibbon:** “The general peace which he maintained during the last fourteen years of his reign was a period of apparent splendor rather than of real prosperity; and the old age of Constantine was disgraced by the opposite yet reconcilable vices of rapaciousness and prodigality.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1872](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1872).

### 12. Crispus — victory and execution

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-crispus -->
<a id="gibbon-04-crispus"></a>

**Q:** Who was Crispus, and what was his fate under Constantine?

**A:** **Constantine’s eldest son**, a Caesar and successful commander whose fleet helped defeat Licinius. Constantine had him executed at **Pola in 326**; the charge and precise circumstances remain uncertain.

**Gibbon:** “The story of these unhappy princes, the nature and evidence of their guilt, the forms of their trial, and the circumstances of their death, were buried in mysterious obscurity;”

**More:** A disputed story links Crispus’s fall to **Fausta, Constantine’s wife and Crispus’s stepmother**: she supposedly accused him of a sexual offence and later died in an overheated bath. The connection is uncertain.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1876](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1876); [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1880](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1880); [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1882](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1882).

### 13. Constantine — ruling after death

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-constantine-death -->
<a id="gibbon-04-constantine-death"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Constantine die, and what extraordinary ceremony followed?

**A:** He died after a **short illness near Nicomedia in 337**. His body was taken to Constantinople, dressed in purple and diadem, and still received the daily homage of officials. His sons inherited the empire.

**Gibbon:** “From motives of policy, this theatrical representation was for some time continued; nor could flattery neglect the opportunity of remarking that Constantine alone, by the peculiar indulgence of Heaven, had reigned after his death.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1888](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1888).

### 14. The inheritance cleared by murder

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-family-massacre -->
<a id="gibbon-04-family-massacre"></a>

**Q:** How did the massacre after Constantine’s death in 337 reshape his intended succession?

**A:** Constantine had raised nephews as well as sons to power. Soldiers killed **male relatives and their supporters**, clearing the way for his three sons. His nephews **Gallus and Julian** survived; his son **Constantius II’s** responsibility remains uncertain.

**Gibbon:** “But this reign could subsist only in empty pageantry; and it was soon discovered that the will of the most absolute monarch is seldom obeyed when his subjects have no longer anything to hope from his favor or to dread from his resentment.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1884](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1884); [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1890](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1890); [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1892](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1892); [DIR: Constantius II, succession](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/constaii.htm).

### 15. The three sons become Augusti

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-three-sons -->
<a id="gibbon-04-three-sons"></a>

**Q:** Who shared the empire after Constantine’s death in 337?

**A:** **Constantine II, Constantius II, and Constans**, his surviving sons, took the title **Augustus**. Constantine II ruled the far West, Constans Italy, Africa and part of Illyricum, and Constantius the East.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1886](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1886); [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1894](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1894); [DIR: Constantius II, division in 337](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/constaii.htm).

### 16. Constantine II — the fatal invasion

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-constantine-ii-end -->
<a id="gibbon-04-constantine-ii-end"></a>

**Q:** How did Constantine II’s reign end?

**A:** In **340**, he invaded the territories of his brother **Constans** and was killed in an ambush near **Aquileia**. Constans absorbed his western dominions.

**Gibbon:** “After the partition of the empire, three years had scarcely elapsed before the sons of Constantine seemed impatient to convince mankind that they were incapable of contenting themselves with the dominions which they were unqualified to govern [March A.D. 340].”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1900](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1900).

### 17. Constans — a birthday coup

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-constans-magnentius -->
<a id="gibbon-04-constans-magnentius"></a>

**Q:** How did Magnentius overthrow Constans?

**A:** In **350**, a conspiracy at **Autun**, disguised as a birthday feast, acclaimed Magnentius emperor. **Constans fled and was killed near the Pyrenees**; the western armies transferred their allegiance to the usurper.

**Gibbon:** “On a sudden the doors were thrown open, and Magnentius, who had retired for a few moments, returned into the apartment, invested with the diadem and purple.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1902](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1902).

### 18. Constantius II — reunification

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-constantius-sole -->
<a id="gibbon-04-constantius-sole"></a>

**Q:** How did Constantius II become sole emperor?

**A:** After Constans’s murder, he overcame **Magnentius** in the civil war of **351–353**, including the costly victory at **Mursa**. Magnentius killed himself in **353**, leaving Constantius sole ruler.

**More:** Vetranio, briefly acclaimed in Illyricum in 350, had already been persuaded to abdicate.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1906](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1906); [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1910](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1910); [Gibbon, Ch. XVIII, L1912](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1912).

## Constantius, Gallus, and the young Julian — XIX

### 19. Who had influence with whom?

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-eusebius -->
<a id="gibbon-04-eusebius"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon convey the power of Eusebius, Constantius II’s chamberlain?

**A:** **Eusebius dominated access to the emperor and the palace.** Gibbon repeats a sarcasm that reverses the proper relation between monarch and servant.

**Gibbon:** “Of these slaves the most distinguished was the chamberlain Eusebius, who ruled the monarch and the palace with such absolute sway that Constantius, according to the sarcasm of an impartial historian, possessed some credit with this haughty favorite.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1927](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1927).

### 20. Gallus — from confinement to Caesar

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-gallus -->
<a id="gibbon-04-gallus"></a>

**Q:** Why did Constantius II elevate Gallus, and how did Gallus’s rule end?

**A:** Constantius made his cousin **Caesar in 351** to govern the East while he fought in the West. Gallus’s violent administration at Antioch and conflict with imperial officials led to his recall and **execution at Pola in 354**.

**Gibbon:** “Transported from a prison to a throne, he possessed neither genius nor application, nor docility to compensate for the want of knowledge and experience.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1929](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1929); [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1931](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1931); [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1941](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1941).

### 21. Julian’s protector

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-eusebia -->
<a id="gibbon-04-eusebia"></a>

**Q:** Who helped save Julian after the execution of his brother Gallus?

**A:** **Eusebia, the wife of Constantius II**. Her intercession helped secure his release from danger at court and his departure to study at Athens; she later supported his appointment as Caesar.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1943](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1943); [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1945](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1945).

### 22. Julian — the reluctant Caesar

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-caesar -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-caesar"></a>

**Q:** When and why did Julian become Caesar?

**A:** **355**, appointed by his cousin **Constantius II** to defend and administer **Gaul**. He also married **Helena, Constantius’s sister**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1945](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1945); [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1949](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1949); [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1951](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1951).

### 23. The philosopher learns war

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-gaul -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-gaul"></a>

**Q:** What established Julian’s reputation in Gaul?

**A:** His campaigns **restored Roman strength against the Franks and Alemanni**, notably the victory at **Strasbourg in 357**. The inexperienced scholar became a successful commander.

**More:** He repaired frontier strongholds and secured supplies for soldiers and civilians.

**Gibbon:** “O Plato, Plato, what a task for a philosopher!”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1965](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1965); [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1967](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1967); [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1971](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1971); [DIR: Julian, The Campaigns in Gaul](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/julian.htm).

### 24. Who will ever be innocent?

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-evidence -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-evidence"></a>

**Q:** What principle did Julian defend when the prosecutor Delphidius complained that denial let defendants escape?

**A:** **An accusation is not proof.** Julian answered the prosecutor’s demand for easy conviction by asking how innocence could survive if mere assertion were enough.

**Gibbon:** “Who will ever be found guilty,” exclaimed the vehement Delphidius, “if it be enough to deny?” “And who,” replied Julian, “will ever be innocent, if it be sufficient to affirm?”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1973](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1973).

### 25. Pressure on the eastern frontier

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-amida -->
<a id="gibbon-04-amida"></a>

**Q:** What Persian success increased the pressure on Constantius II while Julian defended Gaul?

**A:** **Shapur II captured Amida in 359** after a costly siege. Constantius moved east to face renewed Persian attacks, while the empire’s western defence depended heavily on Julian.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1957](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1957); [Gibbon, Ch. XIX, L1959](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1959).

## Constantine and Christianity — XX

### 26. A conversion without a single date

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-conversion -->
<a id="gibbon-04-conversion"></a>

**Q:** What stages mark Constantine’s public adoption of Christianity?

**A:** His **protection, profession, and practice developed gradually**. The victory of 312 and toleration of 313 were major public steps, but he received **baptism only in his last illness in 337**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XX, L1990](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1990).

### 27. The settlement of Milan

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-milan -->
<a id="gibbon-04-milan"></a>

**Q:** What did Constantine and Licinius grant Christians in 313?

**A:** **Freedom of worship and restoration of confiscated church property**, within a declaration of religious liberty for others as well. The agreement is conventionally called the **Edict of Milan**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XX, L1996](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1996); [Gibbon, Ch. XX, L1998](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1998).

### 28. The labarum

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-labarum -->
<a id="gibbon-04-labarum"></a>

**Q:** What was Constantine’s labarum?

**A:** An **imperial military standard bearing the Chi-Rho (☧)**, the first two Greek letters of Christ’s name. It joined the emperor’s victories and authority to the protection of Christ.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XX, L2010](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2010); [Eusebius, Life of Constantine I.31](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/25021.htm).

### 29. Dream and celestial sign

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-vision -->
<a id="gibbon-04-vision"></a>

**Q:** How do the accounts of Constantine’s sign differ between the Christian writer Lactantius and Eusebius of Caesarea, his bishop-biographer?

**A:** **Lactantius reports a dream** before Constantine’s battle with Maxentius in **312**; **Eusebius describes a luminous cross in the sky**, followed by a dream. Gibbon distinguishes these narratives from the standard’s historical use.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XX, L2012](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2012); [Gibbon, Ch. XX, L2014](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2014).

### 30. Belief and advantage

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-faith-interest -->
<a id="gibbon-04-faith-interest"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon reject the claim that Constantine’s Christianity must have been pure calculation?

**A:** **Interest and sincere belief can reinforce each other.** Success, praise, habit, and the conviction of divine favour might turn a useful profession into genuine devotion.

**Gibbon:** “Personal interest is often the standard of our belief, as well as of our practice; and the same motives of temporal advantage which might influence the public conduct and professions of Constantine would insensibly dispose his mind to embrace a religion so propitious to his fame and fortunes.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XX, L2016](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2016).

### 31. The delayed washing of sins

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-baptism -->
<a id="gibbon-04-baptism"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon explain the appeal of delaying baptism until the end of life?

**A:** Baptism was believed to **wash away all earlier sins**, but could not be repeated. Delay could therefore preserve a final absolution while postponing the obligations of a fully initiated Christian.

**Gibbon:** “Future tyrants were encouraged to believe that the innocent blood which they might shed in a long reign would instantly be washed away in the waters of regeneration; and the abuse of religion dangerously undermined the foundations of moral virtue.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XX, L2020](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2020).

### 32. The emperor and the bishops

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-church-state -->
<a id="gibbon-04-church-state"></a>

**Q:** What tension did Constantine’s alliance with the church create?

**A:** Emperors claimed authority to regulate religion, while bishops claimed **a sacred authority distinct from the state**. The ruler who gave the church privileges was spiritually one of its lay members.

**Gibbon:** “The emperor might be saluted as the father of his people, but he owed a filial duty and reverence to the fathers of the church; and the same marks of respect which Constantine had paid to the persons of saints and confessors were soon exacted by the pride of the episcopal order.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XX, L2024](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2024); [Gibbon, Ch. XX, L2026](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2026).

### 33. An election survived

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-church-liberty -->
<a id="gibbon-04-church-liberty"></a>

**Q:** What lost civic privilege does Gibbon find preserved in episcopal elections?

**A:** **Choosing the magistrates one must obey.** Clergy and people participated in selecting their bishop, even after meaningful political election had disappeared from imperial government.

**Gibbon:** “The freedom of election subsisted long after the legal establishment of Christianity; and the subjects of Rome enjoyed in the church the privilege which they had lost in the republic, of choosing the magistrates whom they were bound to obey.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XX, L2030](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2030).

## Nicaea, heresy, and religious power — XXI

### 34. The persecuted become persecutors

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-persecution -->
<a id="gibbon-04-persecution"></a>

**Q:** What reversal followed Constantine’s religious toleration?

**A:** The victorious church gained imperial help against **Christian dissenters**: their privileges were denied, their assemblies prohibited, and their property confiscated.

**Gibbon:** “Some of the penal regulations were copied from the edicts of Diocletian; and this method of conversion was applauded by the same bishops who had felt the hand of oppression and pleaded for the rights of humanity.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2059](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2059).

### 35. The Donatist schism

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-donatists -->
<a id="gibbon-04-donatists"></a>

**Q:** What divided the Donatists from their Catholic opponents in North Africa?

**A:** **Whether clergy accused of betrayal during persecution could hold valid religious authority.** The dispute included bishops accused of surrendering scriptures to Roman officials and their role in the succession at Carthage. Donatists rejected their opponents’ ministry and repeated baptism and ordination.

**Gibbon:** “Notwithstanding this irreconcilable aversion, the two parties, who were mixed and separated in all the cities of Africa, had the same language and manners, the same zeal and learning, the same faith and worship.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2061](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2061); [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2065](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2065).

### 36. Arius — the disputed Son

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-arius -->
<a id="gibbon-04-arius"></a>

**Q:** What central teaching of Arius did the Council of Nicaea reject?

**A:** That the **Son was created by the Father**, exalted above all other creatures but not coeternal with him. **Nicaea in 325** affirmed that the Son was of the **same substance** as the Father.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2085](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2085); [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2091](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2091).

### 37. A difference of substance

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-one-letter -->
<a id="gibbon-04-one-letter"></a>

**Q:** What distinction lay behind “homoousios” and “homoiousios”?

**A:** **Same substance** versus **similar substance**, describing the Son’s relation to the Father. The small difference in spelling marked a serious dispute about divine nature and became a badge of religious allegiance.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2093](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2093).

### 38. Heaven decided at court

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-creeds-power -->
<a id="gibbon-04-creeds-power"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon connect imperial patronage with the shifting fortunes of fourth-century creeds?

**A:** An emperor’s favour could **exile bishops, restore their rivals, and enforce a creed**. Constantine supported Nicaea, later recalled its opponents, and still considered himself its defender; Constantius II opposed Nicene consubstantiality.

**Gibbon:** “But as those princes presumed to extend their despotism over the faith, as well as over the lives and fortunes, of their subjects, the weight of their suffrage sometimes inclined the ecclesiastical balance: and the prerogatives of the King of Heaven were settled, or changed, or modified in the cabinet of an earthly monarch.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2097](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2097); [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2099](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2099); [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2101](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2101); [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2109](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2109).

### 39. Athanasius — the resistant bishop

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-athanasius -->
<a id="gibbon-04-athanasius"></a>

**Q:** Why does Athanasius stand out in the conflict with Constantius II?

**A:** **The bishop of Alexandria defended the Nicene faith despite repeated displacement and exile.** His authority and resistance frustrated the emperor’s attempts to impose religious agreement.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2109](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2109); [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2111](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2111).

### 40. Saints, heretics, and human passions

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-faction -->
<a id="gibbon-04-faction"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon distrust portraits that make one Christian faction wholly saintly and the other wholly wicked?

**A:** **Shared upbringing and similar passions mattered more to conduct than fine doctrinal differences.** Either faction could abuse court favour or popular power.

**Gibbon:** “Our calmer reason will reject such pure and perfect monsters of vice or sanctity, and will impute an equal, or at least an indiscriminate, measure of good and evil to the hostile sectaries who assumed and bestowed the appellations of orthodox and heretics.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2123](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2123).

### 41. The temples remain

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-pagan-survival -->
<a id="gibbon-04-pagan-survival"></a>

**Q:** What happened to public pagan worship under Constantine and his sons?

**A:** It **persisted under growing pressure**. Despite restrictions, attacks on temples, and Christian privilege, many temples, festivals, and sacrifices continued. The transition varied across the empire.

**Gibbon:** “The divisions of Christianity suspended the ruin of Paganism; and the holy war against the Infidels was less vigorously prosecuted by princes and bishops who were more immediately alarmed by the guilt and danger of domestic rebellion.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2125](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2125); [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2127](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2127); [Gibbon, Ch. XXI, L2129](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2129).

## Julian wins the empire — XXII

### 42. Julian — acclaimed at Paris

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-augustus -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-augustus"></a>

**Q:** What precipitated Julian’s proclamation as Augustus in 360?

**A:** **Constantius II ordered Gallic troops east for the Persian war.** Soldiers unwilling to leave Julian and their families acclaimed him at **Paris**, raising him on a shield and using a military collar as a diadem.

**Gibbon:** “The inflexible Caesar sustained till the third hour of the day their prayers, their reproaches, and their menaces; nor did he yield till he had been repeatedly assured that if he wished to live, he must consent to reign.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXII, L2146](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2146); [Gibbon, Ch. XXII, L2148](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2148); [Gibbon, Ch. XXII, L2150](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2150).

### 43. Constantius II — the war that did not happen

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-constantius-death -->
<a id="gibbon-04-constantius-death"></a>

**Q:** How did Constantius II’s death change Julian’s position?

**A:** **Constantius died of illness near Tarsus in November 361**, before their expected civil war. The eastern army accepted Julian, who became **sole emperor**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXII, L2160](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2160).

### 44. Julian’s avarice of time

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-time -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-time"></a>

**Q:** What does Gibbon mean by Julian’s “avarice of time”?

**A:** **He guarded every hour for government or study.** Frugal meals, little sleep, constant work, and impatience with public entertainments gave an unusually full record to a very short reign.

**Gibbon:** “He was soon awakened by the entrance of fresh secretaries, who had slept the preceding day; and his servants were obliged to wait alternately while their indefatigable master allowed himself scarcely any other refreshment than the change of occupation.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXII, L2162](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2162).

### 45. An emptied palace

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-reform -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-reform"></a>

**Q:** What was the strength and the fault of Julian’s reform of the imperial household?

**A:** He **cut court luxury and dismissed a vast staff**, but acted so broadly that faithful and vulnerable servants suffered with the useless. Gibbon admires the economy and criticises its indiscriminate severity.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXII, L2164](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2164).

### 46. The repose of one man

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-spies -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-spies"></a>

**Q:** What contrast does Gibbon draw between Constantius II’s informers and Julian’s reform?

**A:** **The security of one ruler against the insecurity of millions.** Gibbon praises Julian’s attack on a system that bought the emperor’s peace by disturbing his subjects’ lives.

**Gibbon:** “The numerous army of spies, of agents, and informers enlisted by Constantius to secure the repose of one man and to interrupt that of millions was immediately disbanded by his generous successor.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXII, L2166](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2166).

### 47. Worth without the purple

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-verdict -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-verdict"></a>

**Q:** What makes Gibbon’s praise of Julian more than praise of a fortunate prince?

**A:** **His courage, wit, and application could have distinguished him without inherited rank.** Gibbon sees substantial merit beneath the vanity and religious enthusiasm.

**Gibbon:** “The generality of princes, if they were stripped of their purple and cast naked into the world, would immediately sink to the lowest rank of society without a hope of emerging from their obscurity.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXII, L2170](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2170).

## Julian’s religious experiment — XXIII

### 48. Julian — the apostate

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-apostasy -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-apostasy"></a>

**Q:** Why is Julian called “the Apostate”?

**A:** Raised and baptised as a **Christian**, he abandoned Christianity for the worship of the gods. His devotion was concealed under Constantius, then openly professed during his struggle for power.

**Gibbon:** “The names of Christ and of Constantius, the ideas of slavery and of religion, were soon associated in a youthful imagination which was susceptible of the most lively impressions.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2187](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2187); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2195](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2195); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2197](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2197).

### 49. Julian’s philosophers — enthusiasm and imposture

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-theurgy -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-theurgy"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon turn from Julian’s pagan philosophers to “enthusiasm” and “imposture”?

**A:** They practised **theurgy—rituals claiming access to gods and power over nature**. Gibbon sees supposed revelation as a mixture of sincere delusion and deliberate deception, made more troubling by the philosophers’ claims to wisdom.

**Gibbon:** “In every age, the absence of genuine inspiration is supplied by the strong illusions of enthusiasm and the mimic arts of imposture.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2193](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2193).

### 50. Toleration with a political use

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-toleration -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-toleration"></a>

**Q:** What double purpose does Gibbon find in Julian’s religious toleration?

**A:** **Freedom of worship avoided creating Christian martyrs**, while recalling rival Christian leaders revived their quarrels and weakened their unity.

**Gibbon:** “Religious obstinacy is hardened and exasperated by oppression; and as soon as the persecution subsides, those who have yielded are restored as penitents, and those who have resisted are honored as saints and martyrs.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2199](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2199).

### 51. The danger to the cattle

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-sacrifices -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-sacrifices"></a>

**Q:** What joke did Julian’s extravagant sacrifices provoke?

**A:** If he returned victorious from Persia, **there would be no horned cattle left**. His personal economy coexisted with enormous spending on sacrifices and restored temples.

**Gibbon:** “a hundred oxen were frequently sacrificed by Julian on one and the same day; and it soon became a popular jest that if he should return with conquest from the Persian war, the breed of horned cattle must infallibly be extinguished.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2201](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2201).

### 52. Learning from an enemy

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-charity -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-charity"></a>

**Q:** What Christian strength did Julian try to reproduce in pagan religion?

**A:** **Organised charity and disciplined religious leadership.** He wanted pagan priests to win the respect and attachment that the church gained through care for others.

**Gibbon:** “Julian beheld with envy the wise and humane regulations of the church; and he very frankly confesses his intention to deprive the Christians of the applause, as well as advantage, which they had acquired by the exclusive practice of charity and beneficence.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2203](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2203).

### 53. Who may teach Homer?

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-teachers -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-teachers"></a>

**Q:** How did Julian’s educational policy disadvantage Christians?

**A:** In **362**, Christian teachers were excluded from teaching **grammar and rhetoric**. Julian argued that those who rejected the gods of the classical authors should teach Christian scripture instead.

**Gibbon:** “Julian invited the rising generation to resort with freedom to the public schools, in a just confidence that their tender minds would receive the impressions of literature and idolatry.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2215](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2215).

### 54. The unfinished temple

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-jerusalem -->
<a id="gibbon-04-jerusalem"></a>

**Q:** What did Julian attempt at Jerusalem, and why did it matter to his religious programme?

**A:** He sponsored the **rebuilding of the Jewish Temple**. In Gibbon’s interpretation, success would support sacrificial worship and challenge Christian claims about the Temple’s destruction. **The project failed** before his reign ended.

**Gibbon:** “At this important crisis, any singular accident of nature would assume the appearance and produce the effects of a real prodigy.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2207](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2207); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2209](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2209); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2211](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2211).

### 55. Persecution without martyrs

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-persecution -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-persecution"></a>

**Q:** What is Gibbon’s chief qualification to Julian’s reputation for toleration?

**A:** He allowed worship while using **education, patronage, and demands for temple restitution** to weaken Christians. Formal restraint did not prevent officials from harassing them.

**Gibbon:** “I have endeavored faithfully to represent the artful system by which Julian proposed to obtain the effects, without incurring the guilt or reproach, of persecution.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2213](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2213); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2215](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2215); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2217](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2217); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2219](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2219); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIII, L2221](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2221).

## The Persian expedition and Jovian — XXIV

### 56. Marcus Aurelius or Alexander?

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-ambition -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-ambition"></a>

**Q:** What competing ideals does Gibbon place behind Julian’s Persian expedition?

**A:** **The useful ruler and the conquering hero.** He admired **Marcus Aurelius (Gibbon’s “Antoninus”)** for beneficent government, but **Alexander the Great** for military glory. Gibbon makes their competing ideals frame Julian’s Persian expedition of **363**.

**Gibbon:** “In the cool moments of reflection, Julian preferred the useful and benevolent virtues of Antoninus; but his ambitious spirit was inflamed by the glory of Alexander; and he solicited with equal ardor the esteem of the wise and the applause of the multitude [A.D. 362].”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2236](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2236); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2238](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2238); [Gibbon, unabridged Chapter XXIV, opening](https://oll-resources.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/oll3/store/titles/1371/Gibbon_0214-04_EBk_v6.0.pdf); [Julian, The Caesars](https://www.attalus.org/translate/caesars.html).

### 57. The road back from Ctesiphon

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-retreat -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-retreat"></a>

**Q:** Why did Julian’s successful advance into Persia become a crisis?

**A:** He reached **Ctesiphon but did not take it**, then burned most of his supply fleet before marching inland. Persian attacks and scorched fields made provisioning harder; the retreat brought heat, exhaustion, and dwindling food.

**Gibbon:** “But the most certain and irreparable loss of the Romans was that of time.”

**More:** The soldier-historian **Ammianus Marcellinus**, who accompanied the expedition, says only twelve small vessels were retained for building bridges.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2244](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2244); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2246](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2246); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2250](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2250); [Ammianus 24.7–8](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Ammian/24%2A.html#7).

### 58. Julian — the unarmoured emperor

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-death -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-death"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Julian die?

**A:** In **363**, during the retreat from Persia, he rushed into fighting **without his cuirass** and received a fatal spear wound. He died that night, leaving no appointed successor.

**Gibbon:** “His trembling guards, scattered and oppressed by the disorderly throng of friends and enemies, reminded their fearless sovereign that he was without armor; and conjured him to decline the fall of the impending ruin.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2252](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2252); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2254](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2254); [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2256](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2256); [DIR: Julian, The Persian Expedition](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/julian.htm).

### 59. The emperor and the philosopher

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-last-hours -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-last-hours"></a>

**Q:** What “amiable inconsistency” does Gibbon find in Julian’s last hours?

**A:** After speaking calmly about **his own approaching death**, Julian wept on learning that **Anatolius, his friend and a senior officer**, had been killed. Philosophy mastered fear more readily than grief.

**More:** **Sallust**, the senior official in the quotation, brought the news. Julian later discussed the soul with philosophers, recalling the death of Socrates.

**Gibbon:** “After this discourse, which Julian pronounced in a firm and gentle tone of voice, he distributed, by a military testament, the remains of his private fortune; and making some inquiry why Anatolius was not present, he understood from the answer of Sallust that Anatolius was killed; and bewailed, with amiable inconsistency, the loss of his friend.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2254](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2254).

### 60. Julian — two imagined resting places

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-julian-two-graves -->
<a id="gibbon-04-julian-two-graves"></a>

**Q:** What do the two proposed resting places for Julian express in Gibbon’s account?

**A:** His admirers claimed him as both **philosopher and Roman soldier**: Plato’s Academy at Athens suited one identity; burial beside Julius Caesar among Rome’s martial monuments suited the other.

**More:** He was actually buried at **Tarsus in Cilicia**.

**Gibbon:** “The philosopher expressed a very reasonable wish that the disciple of Plato might have reposed amidst the groves of the academy; while the soldier exclaimed, in bolder accents, that the ashes of Julian should have been mingled with those of Caesar, in the field of Mars, and among the ancient monuments of Roman virtue.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2272](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2272).

### 61. Jovian — chosen in danger

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-jovian-accession -->
<a id="gibbon-04-jovian-accession"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Jovian become emperor?

**A:** **363**, acclaimed by the army after Julian’s death in Persia. An officer of the imperial guard, he inherited a hungry army in enemy territory rather than a secure court.

**Gibbon:** “The pride of this unexpected elevation was moderated by the just apprehension that the same day might terminate the life and reign of the new emperor.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2256](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2256).

### 62. The price of escape

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-jovian-treaty -->
<a id="gibbon-04-jovian-treaty"></a>

**Q:** What did Jovian concede to Shapur II to secure peace in 363?

**A:** **Five provinces beyond the Tigris, Nisibis, and other strongholds**, together with abandonment of Roman protection for Armenia. The agreement secured the army’s escape at a heavy territorial and strategic price.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2262](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2262).

### 63. Nisibis — the abandoned threshold

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-nisibis -->
<a id="gibbon-04-nisibis"></a>

**Q:** What made the surrender of Nisibis especially poignant in Gibbon’s narrative?

**A:** The city had **withstood repeated Persian sieges**, and its inhabitants begged to defend themselves. Jovian instead ordered them to leave within **three days** to fulfil the treaty.

**Gibbon:** “The martial youth deserted, with indignant grief, the walls which they had so gloriously defended: the disconsolate mourner dropped a last tear over the tomb of a son or husband, which must soon be profaned by the rude hand of a Barbarian master; and the aged citizen kissed the threshold and clung to the doors of the house where he had passed the cheerful and careless hours of infancy.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXIV, L2268](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2268).

## Valentinian, Valens, and the western succession — XXV

### 64. Jovian — the religious reversal

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-jovian-religion -->
<a id="gibbon-04-jovian-religion"></a>

**Q:** What religious settlement followed Julian’s death?

**A:** **Jovian restored Christian privileges and favoured the Nicene faith**, but also allowed the practice of traditional pagan worship. Julian’s attempted religious restoration lost its imperial support.

**Gibbon:** “The Christians had forgotten the spirit of the gospel; and the Pagans had imbibed the spirit of the church.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2291](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2291); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2293](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2293).

### 65. Jovian — the short reign

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-jovian-death -->
<a id="gibbon-04-jovian-death"></a>

**Q:** How did Jovian’s reign end, and who succeeded him?

**A:** He was **found dead in bed at Dadastana in 364**, after a reign of roughly eight months. The cause is uncertain. Senior officers chose **Valentinian I** at **Nicaea**, and the army acclaimed him.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2295](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2295); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2297](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2297); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2299](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2299).

### 66. A brother or the best Roman?

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-valens-accession -->
<a id="gibbon-04-valens-accession"></a>

**Q:** How did Valens become emperor, and what warning preceded his choice?

**A:** **Valentinian I made his brother Valens co-Augustus in 364**, then entrusted him with the East while retaining the West. An officer had urged him to distinguish family interest from public merit.

**Gibbon:** “Most excellent prince,” said that officer, “if you consider only your family, you have a brother; if you love the republic, look round for the most deserving of the Romans.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2299](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2299); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2301](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2301).

### 67. Procopius — no safety in obscurity

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-procopius -->
<a id="gibbon-04-procopius"></a>

**Q:** Who challenged Valens in 365–366, and what was the outcome?

**A:** **Procopius, a kinsman of Julian**, claimed the throne after insecurity and pursuit drove him from retirement. His initial success ended in defeat and **execution in 366**.

**Gibbon:** “In a moment of impatience and despair, Procopius embarked in a merchant vessel which made sail for Constantinople; and boldly aspired to the rank of a sovereign because he was not allowed to enjoy the security of a subject.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2303](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2303); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2305](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2305); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2307](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2307).

### 68. Fear and fury

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-valentinian-temper -->
<a id="gibbon-04-valentinian-temper"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon distinguish the cruelty of Valens from that of Valentinian I?

**A:** **Valens is driven chiefly by fear; Valentinian by anger.** Suspicion makes one dependent on informers, while unchecked temper turns the other’s severity into arbitrary violence.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2313](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2313).

### 69. The sovereign of the clergy

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-valentinian-toleration -->
<a id="gibbon-04-valentinian-toleration"></a>

**Q:** What does Gibbon most admire in Valentinian I’s religious policy?

**A:** **His relative impartiality and toleration.** A Christian himself, he generally protected different forms of worship and declined to make theological disputes the business of government.

**More:** This was not unrestricted liberty: he also legislated against Manichaeans and Donatists.

**Gibbon:** “The government of the Earth claimed his vigilance and satisfied his ambition; and while he remembered that he was the disciple of the church, he never forgot that he was the sovereign of the clergy.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2317](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2317); [DIR: Valentinian I, Christianity and Legislation](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/vali.htm).

### 70. Valens — the eastern church

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-valens-religion -->
<a id="gibbon-04-valens-religion"></a>

**Q:** How did Valens’s religious policy differ from his brother Valentinian I’s?

**A:** **Valens favoured the Arian side and used imperial power against Nicene opponents**, while Valentinian I generally avoided the eastern doctrinal struggle.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2317](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2317); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2319](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2319); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2321](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2321).

### 71. A bishopric worth converting for

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-roman-bishop -->
<a id="gibbon-04-roman-bishop"></a>

**Q:** What joke did the pagan official Praetextatus make to Damasus, bishop of Rome?

**A:** **Give me the bishopric of Rome, and I will become a Christian.** Gibbon uses the joke to expose the wealth and worldly ambition attached to the office.

**Gibbon:** “Praetextatus was a philosophic Pagan, a man of learning, of taste, and politeness; who disguised a reproach in the form of a jest when he assured Damasus that if he could obtain the bishopric of Rome, he himself would immediately embrace the Christian religion.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2325](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2325).

### 72. Theodosius — service and reward

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-elder-theodosius -->
<a id="gibbon-04-elder-theodosius"></a>

**Q:** Who was the elder Theodosius, and what contrast marks his career in Gibbon?

**A:** **A general who restored Roman control in Britain and suppressed rebellion in Africa**, and the father of the future emperor Theodosius I. Despite his services, he was executed after Valentinian I’s death.

**Gibbon:** “The prince who refuses to be the judge instructs the people to consider him as the accomplice of his ministers.”

**More:** The corrupt African commander **Romanus** escaped punishment. His earlier protection at court prompts Gibbon’s warning about a ruler who refuses to judge his officials.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2333](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2333); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2335](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2335); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2337](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2337); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2341](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2341).

### 73. Valentinian I — death in anger

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-valentinian-death -->
<a id="gibbon-04-valentinian-death"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Valentinian I die?

**A:** In **375**, while angrily receiving **Quadi envoys** at Brigetio on the Danube, he suffered a **fatal stroke**. His sons **Gratian and Valentinian II** shared the western succession.

**More:** The Quadi had protested Roman fortification on their territory; their king had then been killed by Romans.

**Gibbon:** “His eyes, his voice, his color, his gestures, expressed the violence of his ungoverned fury; and while his whole frame was agitated with convulsive passion, a large blood vessel suddenly burst in his body; and Valentinian fell speechless into the arms of his attendants.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2363](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2363); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2365](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2365); [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2367](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2367); [DIR: Valentinian I, The Final Campaign](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/vali.htm).

### 74. Gratian and the child emperor

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-western-sons -->
<a id="gibbon-04-western-sons"></a>

**Q:** How did Gratian and Valentinian II come to share the West?

**A:** **Gratian had been co-Augustus since 367**. After their father **Valentinian I’s** death in **375**, the army proclaimed his four-year-old half-brother **Valentinian II**. Gratian accepted the choice rather than begin a civil war.

**More:** **Justina** was Valentinian II’s mother and Valentinian I’s widow.

**Gibbon:** “He cheerfully accepted the choice of the army; declared that he should always consider the son of Justina as a brother, not as a rival; and advised the empress with her son Valentinian to fix their residence at Milan, in the fair and peaceful province of Italy; while he assumed the more arduous command of the countries beyond the Alps.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXV, L2367](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2367); [DIR: Gratian](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/gratian.htm).

## The Gothic crisis and the end of Volume II — XXVI

### 75. A sinking empire and a sinking world

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-earthquake -->
<a id="gibbon-04-earthquake"></a>

**Q:** What does Gibbon argue after describing the great earthquake of 365?

**A:** Natural disaster encouraged people to imagine **the end of the world alongside the decline of Rome**. Gibbon turns attention back to the greater, sustained harm caused by human conflict.

**Gibbon:** “Without presuming to discuss the truth or propriety of these lofty speculations, the historian may content himself with an observation, which seems to be justified by experience, that man has much more to fear from the passions of his fellow-creatures than from the convulsions of the elements.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2382](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2382).

### 76. The refugees at the Danube

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-goths-danube -->
<a id="gibbon-04-goths-danube"></a>

**Q:** Why did Goths seek entry to the empire in 376, and why did Valens admit them?

**A:** **Hunnic attacks displaced them**; they asked for land and safety. Valens accepted the prospect of **new cultivators and military manpower** and ordered preparations for their crossing and support.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2394](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2394).

### 77. Admission becomes revolt

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-gothic-revolt -->
<a id="gibbon-04-gothic-revolt"></a>

**Q:** How did the Gothic settlement south of the Danube turn into war?

**A:** **Roman commanders exploited and failed to provision the newcomers**. Hunger and abuse broke the arrangement; the Goths revolted, defeated local Roman forces, and were joined by other armed groups.

**More:** The contemporary historian **Ammianus Marcellinus** describes officials selling dogs as food to starving Goths in exchange for enslaved people—including the Goths’ own children.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2394](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2394); [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2396](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2396); [DIR: Valens, The Gothic Crisis](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/valens.htm); [Ammianus 31.4–5](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Ammian/31%2A.html#4).

### 78. Adrianople — choosing battle too soon

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-adrianople-decision -->
<a id="gibbon-04-adrianople-decision"></a>

**Q:** Why did Valens fight at Adrianople in 378 without waiting for Gratian?

**A:** He **underestimated the Gothic force** and hoped to win the glory himself before **Gratian, his nephew and western co-emperor**, arrived. Encouraging reports and overconfidence favoured an immediate battle.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2404](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2404); [Ammianus 31.12](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Ammian/31%2A.html#12).

### 79. Adrianople — the fatal battle

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-adrianople -->
<a id="gibbon-04-adrianople"></a>

**Q:** What happened at Adrianople on 9 August 378?

**A:** **Valens’s army was crushed by the Goths under Fritigern and their allies**, and the emperor was killed. Roman cavalry withdrew; the surrounded infantry could neither manoeuvre nor escape. Reinforcements from the western emperor **Gratian** had not arrived.

**Gibbon:** “the Roman cavalry fled; the infantry was abandoned, surrounded, and cut in pieces.”

**More:** Valens’s body was never securely identified. One account has the wounded emperor burned in a cottage by Goths who did not know he was inside.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2404](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2404); [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2412](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2412); [Ammianus 31.13](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Ammian/31%2A.html#13).

### 80. The fish and the Gothic army

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-fish-danube -->
<a id="gibbon-04-fish-danube"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon puncture the Christian writer Jerome’s picture of total devastation by the Goths?

**A:** Jerome’s lament extends destruction even to **wild animals, birds, and fish**. Gibbon answers that wildlife could flourish when human cultivation and hunting declined.

**Gibbon:** “The various tribes that people the air, or the waters, are still less connected with the fate of the human species; and it is highly probable that the fish of the Danube would have felt more terror and distress from the approach of a voracious pike than from the hostile inroad of a Gothic army.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2408](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2408).

### 81. The limits of public safety

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-gothic-massacre -->
<a id="gibbon-04-gothic-massacre"></a>

**Q:** What Roman atrocity after Adrianople prompts Gibbon to question necessity as an excuse?

**A:** **Julius, a senior commander in the East, arranged the slaughter of Gothic youths** gathered under the promise of gifts. Suspicion of a future revolt served as the justification.

**Gibbon:** “The urgent consideration of the public safety may undoubtedly authorize the violation of every positive law. How far that, or any other, consideration may operate to dissolve the natural obligations of humanity and justice is a doctrine of which I still desire to remain ignorant.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2410](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2410).

### 82. Theodosius I — the eastern rescue

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-theodosius-accession -->
<a id="gibbon-04-theodosius-accession"></a>

**Q:** When and why did Theodosius I become emperor?

**A:** **Gratian appointed him eastern Augustus in 379**, after Valens’s death, to restore the eastern army and conduct the Gothic war. He was the son of the general who had recovered Britain and Africa, then been executed.

**Gibbon:** “What confidence must have been reposed in his integrity, since Gratian could trust that a pious son would forgive, for the sake of the republic, the murder of his father!”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2412](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2412); [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2414](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2414).

### 83. Recovery without a second great battle

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-theodosius-recovery -->
<a id="gibbon-04-theodosius-recovery"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon describe Theodosius’s recovery from Adrianople?

**A:** **Patient restoration of confidence, discipline, and fortified positions**, followed by limited attacks under favourable conditions. Negotiation and circumstance mattered more than a single decisive revenge.

**Gibbon:** “The deliverance and peace of the Roman provinces was the work of prudence rather than of valor: the prudence of Theodosius was seconded by fortune: and the emperor never failed to seize, and to improve, every favorable circumstance [A.D. 379–382].”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2416](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2416); [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2418](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2418).

### 84. The Gothic settlement — strength and dependence

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-gothic-settlement -->
<a id="gibbon-04-gothic-settlement"></a>

**Q:** What bargain, and what danger, does Gibbon see in Theodosius’s settlement of the Goths?

**A:** **Land and support in return for military service** helped end the war by **382**. Gibbon sees the continued cohesion of Gothic communities and troops as a source of dangerous imperial dependence.

**Gibbon:** “the public safety seemed to depend on the life and abilities of a single man.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2418](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2418); [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2420](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2420); [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2422](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2422).

### 85. Taking leave of a guide

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-ammianus -->
<a id="gibbon-04-ammianus"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon pause over the end of the soldier-historian Ammianus Marcellinus’s history?

**A:** Ammianus ends with **Valens’s death in 378**. Gibbon loses a detailed contemporary narrative he trusts and must rely more heavily on fragments, panegyric, and religiously partisan accounts.

**Gibbon:** “Conscious of these disadvantages, which will continue to involve a considerable portion of the decline and fall of the Roman empire, I shall proceed with doubtful and timorous steps.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2416](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2416).

### 86. The surviving empire — the returning problem

<!-- ID: gibbon-04-closing-dependence -->
<a id="gibbon-04-closing-dependence"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon’s ending to Volume II return to the political weakness of the Antonine age?

**A:** The empire had **survived, reunited, and reorganised**, yet its safety again appeared to depend on an exceptional ruler—**Theodosius I**. Recovery under one good emperor still offered no durable protection against the next bad reign.

**Gibbon:** “They must often have recollected the instability of a happiness which depended on the character of a single man. … the public safety seemed to depend on the life and abilities of a single man.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. III, L730](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L730); [Gibbon, Ch. XXVI, L2422](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L2422).
