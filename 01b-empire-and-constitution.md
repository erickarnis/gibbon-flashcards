# Gibbon — The empire behind the emperors

Chapters I–III. Companion to the opening emperors; questions on institutions, society, and Gibbon's political imagination. Dates are AD unless marked BC.

## Arms and conquest

### 1. Augustus — knowing where to stop

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-augustus-limits -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-augustus-limits"></a>

**Q:** Why did Augustus recommend limits to Roman expansion?

**A:** Further conquest offered **diminishing rewards and increasing dangers**. Distant wars were harder to win; distant provinces were harder to hold.

**Gibbon:** “in the prosecution of remote wars, the undertaking became every day more difficult, the event more doubtful, and the possession more precarious, and less beneficial.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. I, L560](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L560).

<!-- Tags: emperor::augustus kind::policy -->

### 2. Britain — courage without union

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-britain-division -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-britain-division"></a>

**Q:** What political weakness, rather than lack of courage, explains Britain's conquest in Gibbon's account?

**A:** The British peoples **failed to unite**. Their separate resistance allowed Rome to defeat them in succession.

**Gibbon:** “The various tribes of Britain possessed valor without conduct, and the love of freedom without the spirit of union.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. I, L564](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L564).

<!-- Tags: kind::conquest kind::theme -->

### 3. The legion — a sacred standard

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-legion-eagle -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-legion-eagle"></a>

**Q:** Why was abandoning a Roman legion's eagle more than a military disgrace?

**A:** The standard carried **religious as well as military loyalty**. Losing it violated the soldier's sacred attachment to his legion and his oath of service.

**Gibbon:** “The golden eagle which glittered in the front of the legion was the object of their fondest devotion; nor was it esteemed less impious than it was ignominious to abandon that sacred ensign in the hour of danger.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. I, L578](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L578).

<!-- Tags: kind::army kind::religion -->

### 4. The legion — practice before courage

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-legion-exercise -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-legion-exercise"></a>

**Q:** How did Roman training turn courage into dependable military strength?

**A:** Through **continuous, demanding practice**, even in peace: recruits and veterans rehearsed movement, endurance, and weapons until battle drew on trained habits.

**More:** Gibbon describes practice weapons twice the weight of those used in action, and covered spaces for winter exercises.

**Gibbon:** “In the midst of peace, the Roman troops familiarized themselves with the practice of war; and it is prettily remarked by an ancient historian who had fought against them that the effusion of blood was the only circumstance which distinguished a field of battle from a field of exercise.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. I, L580](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L580).

## Becoming Roman

### 5. Conquest followed by incorporation

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-citizenship-integration -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-citizenship-integration"></a>

**Q:** What two practices helped turn conquered provincials into Romans?

**A:** **Planting colonies and extending citizenship**. Settlers carried Roman institutions into the provinces, while admission to Roman rights gave local ambition a stake in the empire.

**Gibbon:** “The grandsons of the Gauls who had besieged Julius Caesar in Alesia commanded legions, governed provinces, and were admitted into the senate of Rome.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. II, L617](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L617); [L619](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L619).

<!-- Tags: kind::citizenship kind::institution -->

### 6. The gods become citizens

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-gods-citizenship -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-gods-citizenship"></a>

**Q:** What does Gibbon's image of Rome as the world's common temple describe?

**A:** Rome's **absorption of foreign cults**, as the religions of its subjects found a place in the capital.

**Gibbon:** “Rome gradually became the common temple of her subjects; and the freedom of the city was bestowed on all the gods of mankind.”

**More:** Incorporation was uneven: the Egyptian cults of Isis and Serapis also faced attempts at suppression.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. II, L611](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L611).

<!-- Tags: kind::religion kind::theme -->

### 7. Slavery — the dangerous uniform

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-slaves-uniform -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-slaves-uniform"></a>

**Q:** In the proposal reported by Gibbon, why was distinctive clothing for enslaved people rejected?

**A:** It might reveal **how numerous they were**, making the masters' security more precarious.

**Gibbon:** “It was once proposed to discriminate the slaves by a peculiar habit; but it was justly apprehended that there might be some danger in acquainting them with their own numbers.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. II, L629](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L629).

<!-- Tags: kind::slavery kind::anecdote -->

## The works of peace

### 8. Magnificence for the public

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-public-monuments -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-public-monuments"></a>

**Q:** What makes Roman building especially significant to Gibbon beyond its scale and beauty?

**A:** Its **public use**, often supported by private wealth: buildings and infrastructure served the community as well as displaying the patron's magnificence.

**Gibbon:** “Many of those works were erected at private expense, and almost all were intended for public benefit.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. II, L635](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L635); [L637](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L637).

<!-- Tags: kind::architecture kind::society -->

### 9. Roads — the purpose beneath the benefit

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-roads-conquest -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-roads-conquest"></a>

**Q:** What original purpose lay beneath the Roman roads' benefits for travel and commerce?

**A:** **Military movement and control**. Roads connected the provinces, but first made them accessible to the legions and imperial authority.

**Gibbon:** “nor was any country considered as completely subdued till it had been rendered, in all its parts, pervious to the arms and authority of the conqueror.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. II, L639](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L639).

<!-- Tags: kind::infrastructure kind::army -->

### 10. Luxury — wealth put back into circulation

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-luxury-circulation -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-luxury-circulation"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon give luxury an economic defence despite its moral reputation?

**A:** The rich person's spending **supports artisans and circulates wealth**. Goods made in the provinces could bring back money previously extracted through Roman rule.

**Gibbon:** “The diligent mechanic and the skillful artist who have obtained no share in the division of the earth receive a voluntary tax from the possessors of land;”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. II, L643](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L643).

<!-- Tags: kind::economy kind::argument -->

### 11. Learning without invention

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-literature-imitation -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-literature-imitation"></a>

**Q:** What distinction underlies Gibbon's criticism of literature in the age of the Antonines?

**A:** **Preserving and imitating learning is not the same as creating it.** He sees reverence for established models as stifling original thought.

**Gibbon:** “A cloud of critics, of compilers, of commentators, darkened the face of learning, and the decline of genius was soon followed by the corruption of taste.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. II, L651](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L651).

<!-- Tags: kind::literature kind::argument -->

## Augustus and the appearance of liberty

### 12. The Senate — dignity without independence

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-senate-selection -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-senate-selection"></a>

**Q:** What contradiction does Gibbon find in Augustus's reform of the Senate?

**A:** Augustus restored its **prestige while controlling its membership**. A body selected by the ruler could scarcely restrain him.

**Gibbon:** “The principles of a free constitution are irrecoverably lost when the legislative power is nominated by the executive.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. III, L672](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L672).

<!-- Tags: emperor::augustus kind::constitution -->

### 13. The offer to resign

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-augustus-resignation -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-augustus-resignation"></a>

**Q:** How did Augustus turn an apparent resignation into a renewed grant of power?

**A:** He offered to restore the republic; the Senate **begged him to retain command**, and he accepted a nominally limited term. Gibbon presents the exchange as a carefully staged performance.

**Gibbon:** “It was dangerous to trust the sincerity of Augustus; to seem to distrust it was still more dangerous.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. III, L674](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L674); [L676](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L676).

<!-- Tags: emperor::augustus kind::constitution kind::scene -->

### 14. Provinces — honour and force divided

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-provinces-division -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-provinces-division"></a>

**Q:** Why did Augustus's division of provinces with the Senate preserve his supremacy?

**A:** He retained the **frontiers and their armies**, while the Senate received more peaceful provinces. The concession distributed honour without surrendering the main instruments of force.

**Gibbon:** “The former were attended by lictors, the latter by soldiers.”

**More:** The Senate’s proconsuls travelled with ceremonial attendants; the emperor’s provincial lieutenants commanded soldiers.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. III, L682](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L682).

<!-- Tags: emperor::augustus kind::constitution kind::army -->

### 15. Powers once divided

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-powers-combination -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-powers-combination"></a>

**Q:** How did combining republican offices change their ability to restrain one another?

**A:** Powers once divided among **colleagues, annual officeholders, and opposing magistracies** became concentrated for life in the emperor. Republican titles survived while their checks disappeared.

**Gibbon:** “It was more agreeable to his temper, as well as to his policy, to reign under the venerable names of ancient magistracy, and artfully to collect in his own person all the scattered rays of civil jurisdiction.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. III, L686](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L686).

<!-- Tags: emperor::augustus kind::constitution -->

### 16. Augustus and Caesar — titles outlive a family

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-imperial-titles -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-imperial-titles"></a>

**Q:** How did the meanings of Augustus and Caesar develop into a distinction between ruler and heir?

**A:** **Augustus** began as Octavian's personal honorific; **Caesar** was an inherited family name. They became imperial titles, with Augustus marking the sovereign and Caesar increasingly marking the designated successor.

**More:** Octavian received the title Augustus in **27 BC**. Gibbon places the established use of Caesar for the presumptive heir at least as early as Hadrian.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. III, L700](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L700).

<!-- Tags: kind::titles kind::succession -->

### 17. A republic of forty-eight hours

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-claudius-senate -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-claudius-senate"></a>

**Q:** What defeated the Senate's attempt to recover republican authority after Caligula's assassination?

**A:** The **Praetorians chose Claudius**. While the Senate debated restoration, armed support settled the succession and forced it to accept another emperor.

**Gibbon:** “But while they deliberated, the Praetorian guards had resolved.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. III, L706](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L706).

<!-- Tags: emperor::claudius kind::succession kind::scene -->

### 18. An empire without an outside

<!-- ID: gibbon-01b-world-prison -->
<a id="gibbon-01b-world-prison"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon describe the Roman world under a tyrant as a prison?

**A:** Its very **size denied the victim a practical refuge**. Where rival states might shelter an exile, one master's authority reached across the Roman world.

**Gibbon:** “But the empire of the Romans filled the world, and when the empire fell into the hands of a single person, the world became a safe and dreary prison for his enemies.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. III, L740](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L740).
