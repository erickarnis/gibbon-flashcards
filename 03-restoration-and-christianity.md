# Gibbon — Restoration, Tetrarchy, and Christianity

Chapters XI–XVI of Mueller’s abridgment. All dates are AD. Markdown review draft.

## Claudius and Aurelian

### 1. Claudius — the Gothic victor

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-claudius-goths -->
<a id="gibbon-03-claudius-goths"></a>

**Q:** What earned Claudius II the name Gothicus?

**A:** His victory over the **Goths at Naissus in 269**, followed by a campaign that confined survivors in the Balkan mountains, where hunger and disease completed the work of battle.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XI, L1260](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1260); [DIR: Claudius II Gothicus](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/claudgot.htm).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 2. Claudius — the conqueror conquered

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-claudius-death -->
<a id="gibbon-03-claudius-death"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Claudius II die?

**A:** In **270**, of the epidemic that also ravaged the Goths, at **Sirmium**. His brother **Quintillus** briefly claimed the succession; Aurelian secured the throne.

**Gibbon:** “The pestilence which swept away such numbers of the Barbarians at length proved fatal to their conqueror.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1262](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1262); [DIR: Aurelian](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/aurelian.htm).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 3. Aurelian — accession

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-aurelian-accession -->
<a id="gibbon-03-aurelian-accession"></a>

**Q:** How did Aurelian become emperor in 270?

**A:** The **Danubian army proclaimed him** after Claudius II died. The rival reign of Claudius’s brother **Quintillus** quickly collapsed, leaving Aurelian in possession.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1264](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1264); [DIR: Aurelian](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/aurelian.htm).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 4. Aurelian — whose tears pay the army?

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-aurelian-discipline -->
<a id="gibbon-03-aurelian-discipline"></a>

**Q:** What principle governs the military discipline attributed to Aurelian?

**A:** Soldiers must live on their public allowance and the spoils of enemies, **not plunder the provincials they defend**. Gibbon pairs this rule with severe punishments.

**Gibbon:** “their wealth should be collected from the spoils of the enemy, not from the tears of the provincials.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1266](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1266).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 5. Aurelian — the melancholy walls

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-aurelian-walls -->
<a id="gibbon-03-aurelian-walls"></a>

**Q:** What did Aurelian’s fortification of Rome signify to Gibbon?

**A:** Rome itself now needed walls against invasion: **defending the capital exposed the insecurity of the empire**. Aurelian began the new circuit after German incursions into Italy.

**Gibbon:** “It was a great but a melancholy labor, since the defense of the capital betrayed the decline of the monarchy.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1276](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1276).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 6. Aurelian — a useful retreat

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-aurelian-dacia -->
<a id="gibbon-03-aurelian-dacia"></a>

**Q:** Why did Aurelian withdraw from Dacia beyond the Danube?

**A:** To **shorten and strengthen the frontier**, accepting the loss of Trajan’s province rather than defending an exposed conquest. Gibbon praises the practical judgment behind an apparent humiliation.

**Gibbon:** “His manly judgment convinced him of the solid advantages, and taught him to despise the seeming disgrace, of thus contracting the frontiers of the monarchy.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1268](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1268).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 7. Zenobia — queen of the East

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-zenobia-power -->
<a id="gibbon-03-zenobia-power"></a>

**Q:** Who was Zenobia, and how did she become Rome’s eastern rival?

**A:** The **widow of Odenathus**, ruler of Palmyra. After his assassination in **267**, she controlled his eastern dominions and extended Palmyrene power into **Egypt**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1286](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1286); [Gibbon, L1288](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1288).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 8. Aurelian — restoring the Roman world

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-aurelian-reunification -->
<a id="gibbon-03-aurelian-reunification"></a>

**Q:** Which two breakaway powers did Aurelian overthrow to reunite the empire?

**A:** **Zenobia’s Palmyrene empire in 272**, and **Tetricus’s Gallic empire in 274**. A renewed revolt brought the destruction of Palmyra in **273**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1264](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1264); [Gibbon, L1300](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1300); [DIR: Aurelian](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/aurelian.htm).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 9. Zenobia — the weight of victory

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-zenobia-captive -->
<a id="gibbon-03-zenobia-captive"></a>

**Q:** What reversal does Gibbon make memorable in Zenobia’s appearance at Aurelian’s triumph?

**A:** The queen who had hoped to enter Rome in triumph **walks before her own chariot as a captive**, burdened with gold and jewels.

**Gibbon:** “She preceded on foot the magnificent chariot in which she once hoped to enter the gates of Rome.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1302](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1302).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 10. Aurelian — the Sun at Rome

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-aurelian-sun -->
<a id="gibbon-03-aurelian-sun"></a>

**Q:** Which god received Aurelian’s conspicuous devotion after the reunification of the empire?

**A:** **Sol, the Sun**. Aurelian dedicated a magnificent Roman temple and enriched it with eastern spoils in **274**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1306](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1306); [DIR: Aurelian](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/aurelian.htm).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 11. Aurelian — murdered through fear

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-aurelian-death -->
<a id="gibbon-03-aurelian-death"></a>

**Q:** When and through what plot did Aurelian’s reign end?

**A:** He was **murdered in 275**, near Byzantium, while preparing a Persian campaign. In the account Gibbon follows, a threatened secretary forged a list of officers marked for execution; their fear turned them into assassins.

**Gibbon:** “He there experienced that the most absolute power is a weak defense against the effects of despair.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1310](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1310).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

## Tacitus, Probus, and the house of Carus

### 12. Tacitus — accession

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-tacitus-accession -->
<a id="gibbon-03-tacitus-accession"></a>

**Q:** Who succeeded Aurelian, and when?

**A:** **Tacitus, in 275**, chosen by the army and confirmed by the Senate. His reign briefly revived hopes of senatorial influence.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1329](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1329); [DIR: Tacitus](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/tacitus.htm).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 13. Tacitus — the Senate’s last blaze

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-tacitus-senate -->
<a id="gibbon-03-tacitus-senate"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon portray the Senate’s revival under the emperor Tacitus?

**A:** As a **brief recovery without independent strength**: the Senate could acclaim a ruler, but its hopes still depended on the army.

**Gibbon:** “The expiring senate displayed a sudden luster, blazed for a moment and was extinguished forever.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1339](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1339).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 14. Tacitus and Florianus — the succession of 276

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-tacitus-florianus -->
<a id="gibbon-03-tacitus-florianus"></a>

**Q:** How did the deaths of Tacitus and Florianus clear the way for Probus?

**A:** **Tacitus died in 276** during an eastern campaign; illness and murder are rival accounts. His brother **Florianus** claimed the throne, but his troops killed him during the contest with **Probus**, whom the eastern army supported.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1343](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1343); [Gibbon, L1345](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1345); [DIR: Tacitus](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/tacitus.htm).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 15. Probus — the sword and the sceptre

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-probus-sword -->
<a id="gibbon-03-probus-sword"></a>

**Q:** What lesson does Gibbon draw from the Senate’s military exclusion under Probus?

**A:** A body that leaves **armed power entirely to others** cannot retain sovereignty, however respectfully an emperor addresses it. Probus gained the throne in **276** through his soldiers, then sought senatorial approval.

**Gibbon:** “They soon experienced that those who refuse the sword must renounce the scepter.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1349](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1349).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 16. Probus — rescuing Gaul

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-probus-gaul -->
<a id="gibbon-03-probus-gaul"></a>

**Q:** What was Probus’s great western achievement?

**A:** He **drove German invaders out of Gaul in 277**, recovering its cities and restoring the frontier.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1353](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1353).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 17. Probus — soldiers as husbandmen

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-probus-labor -->
<a id="gibbon-03-probus-labor"></a>

**Q:** How did Probus try to prevent military disorder in peacetime?

**A:** He kept soldiers at **useful work**: canals, drainage, buildings, and vineyards. Labour was meant to prevent the idleness from which mutiny grew.

**Gibbon:** “An army thus employed constituted perhaps the most useful as well as the bravest portion of Roman subjects.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1359](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1359).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 18. Probus’s triumph — refusing the spectacle

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-probus-gladiators -->
<a id="gibbon-03-probus-gladiators"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon admire the gladiators who rebelled during the celebrations of Probus’s triumph?

**A:** They refused to die for the crowd’s amusement, killed their keepers, and fought until overwhelmed. Gibbon honours their **resistance to degradation**, even amid his praise of the victorious emperor.

**Gibbon:** “Disdaining to shed their blood for the amusement of the populace, they killed their keepers, broke from the place of their confinement, and filled the streets of Rome with blood and confusion. After an obstinate resistance, they were overpowered and cut in pieces by the regular forces; but they obtained at least an honorable death and the satisfaction of a just revenge.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1357](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1357); [Gibbon, Ch. XII, L1357](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1357).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 19. Probus — killed by his labourers

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-probus-death -->
<a id="gibbon-03-probus-death"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Probus die, and who succeeded him?

**A:** In **282**, soldiers mutinied during drainage work near **Sirmium** and murdered him. **Carus**, his Praetorian prefect, succeeded.

**Gibbon:** “The rage of the troops subsided as soon as it had been gratified.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1361](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1361); [Gibbon, L1363](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1363).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 20. Carus — victory and mysterious death

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-carus-persia -->
<a id="gibbon-03-carus-persia"></a>

**Q:** What ended Carus’s victorious Persian campaign?

**A:** His **death in 283**, after the capture of **Ctesiphon**. The cause remains uncertain. The army withdrew under his son **Numerian**.

**More:** An ancient report attributed his death to a lightning strike.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1371](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1371); [Gibbon, L1373](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1373); [DIR: Carus](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/carus.htm).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 21. Carinus and Numerian — an unequal promotion

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-carus-sons -->
<a id="gibbon-03-carus-sons"></a>

**Q:** How did Carus’s sons acquire imperial power?

**A:** **Carinus** became Augustus in **283**, while Carus was still alive, and governed in the West. **Numerian** accompanied his father east as Caesar and became Augustus after Carus’s death that year.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1367](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1367); [DIR: Carinus](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/carinus.htm); [DIR: Numerian](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/numerian.htm).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 22. Numerian — an accusation settled by a sword

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-numerian-aper -->
<a id="gibbon-03-numerian-aper"></a>

**Q:** How did Numerian’s death open the throne to Diocletian in 284?

**A:** Numerian died on the army’s return from Persia, and his death was concealed. The troops chose **Diocletian**, who accused **Aper, the Praetorian prefect and Numerian’s father-in-law**, and killed him before he could defend himself. Aper’s guilt was not established.

**Gibbon:** “A charge supported by such decisive proof was admitted without contradiction, and the legions, with repeated acclamations, acknowledged the justice and authority of the emperor Diocletian.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1383](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1383).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 23. Carinus — the end of the dynasty

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-carinus-end -->
<a id="gibbon-03-carinus-end"></a>

**Q:** How did Carinus’s rivalry with Diocletian end?

**A:** Carinus **died during the campaign at the Margus in 285**, leaving Diocletian master of the empire.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1385](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1385); [DIR: Carinus](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/carinus.htm).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

## Diocletian and the Tetrarchy

### 24. Diocletian — a colleague for the West

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-maximian-association -->
<a id="gibbon-03-maximian-association"></a>

**Q:** Why, and when, did Diocletian associate Maximian in imperial power?

**A:** To provide an emperor and commander for the **West** while he directed the East. Maximian became **Caesar in 285** and **Augustus in 286**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1404](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1404).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 25. Diocletian — government by four

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-tetrarchy -->
<a id="gibbon-03-tetrarchy"></a>

**Q:** What was the Tetrarchy established in 293?

**A:** Two senior **Augusti**, Diocletian and Maximian, assisted by two junior **Caesars**, Galerius and Constantius I. Each had regional responsibilities, but all ruled one Roman empire.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1406](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1406).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 26. Diocletian — a planned succession

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-tetrarchy-succession -->
<a id="gibbon-03-tetrarchy-succession"></a>

**Q:** In Gibbon’s account, how was the Tetrarchy meant to make imperial succession orderly?

**A:** The **Caesars would succeed the Augusti**, then appoint new Caesars. It joined military preparation to a designated succession rather than leaving every vacancy to civil war.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1444](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1444).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 27. The Bagaudae — rebellion from below

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-bagaudae -->
<a id="gibbon-03-bagaudae"></a>

**Q:** Who were the Bagaudae suppressed by Maximian in Gaul?

**A:** Insurgents whom Gibbon presents as **oppressed peasants driven into revolt** by landlords, taxation, soldiers, and invasion. Maximian’s disciplined army crushed their resistance.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1410](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1410); [Gibbon, L1412](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1412).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 28. Constantius I — Britain recovered

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-britain-recovered -->
<a id="gibbon-03-britain-recovered"></a>

**Q:** Who restored Britain to central imperial rule in 296?

**A:** **Constantius I**, the western Caesar, recovered the island from the breakaway regime founded by **Carausius, a Roman naval commander**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1414](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1414); [Gibbon, L1416](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1416).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 29. Galerius — the eastern settlement

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-persian-settlement -->
<a id="gibbon-03-persian-settlement"></a>

**Q:** What did Rome gain from the Persian settlement of 298–299 after Galerius defeated Narses (Narseh), the Persian king?

**A:** **Mesopotamia and five provinces beyond the Tigris**, with **King Tiridates restored in Armenia under Roman supremacy**. The settlement strengthened Rome’s eastern frontier and influence in the Caucasus.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1432](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1432); [Ursula Weber, Encyclopaedia Iranica: Narseh](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/narseh-sasanian-king/); [A. Shapur Shahbazi, Encyclopaedia Iranica: Sasanian Dynasty](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sasanian-dynasty/?generate_pdf=1).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 30. Diocletian — emperors away from Rome

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-provincial-courts -->
<a id="gibbon-03-provincial-courts"></a>

**Q:** Which residences express the movement of imperial government away from Rome under Diocletian?

**A:** **Nicomedia** for Diocletian and **Milan** for Maximian: positions better suited to directing the eastern and northern frontiers. Rome retained prestige while losing the regular presence of the court.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1436](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1436).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 31. Diocletian — displaying absolute power

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-dominate-theater -->
<a id="gibbon-03-dominate-theater"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon contrast the political theatre of Augustus and Diocletian?

**A:** Augustus **disguised** monarchy beneath republican forms; Diocletian **displayed** it through court ceremony, seclusion, costly dress, and prostration.

**Gibbon:** “It was the aim of the one to disguise, and the object of the other to display, the unbounded power which the emperors possessed over the Roman world.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1442](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1442).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 32. Diocletian — the price of security

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-administration-cost -->
<a id="gibbon-03-administration-cost"></a>

**Q:** What trade-off does Gibbon see in Diocletian’s expanded administration?

**A:** Dividing authority made government **more secure but more cumbersome and expensive**. More courts and offices meant heavier taxation.

**Gibbon:** “He multiplied the wheels of the machine of government and rendered its operations less rapid but more secure.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1444](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1444); [Gibbon, L1446](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1446).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 33. Diocletian — the voluntary exit

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-abdication -->
<a id="gibbon-03-abdication"></a>

**Q:** When did Diocletian and Maximian abdicate, and who replaced them?

**A:** In **305**, both Augusti resigned. Their Caesars, **Constantius I and Galerius**, became Augusti; **Severus II and Maximin Daia** became the new Caesars.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1450](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1450); [Gibbon, L1473](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1473); [Gibbon, L1477](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1477).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 34. Diocletian — cabbages instead of empire

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-cabbages -->
<a id="gibbon-03-cabbages"></a>

**Q:** How does the cabbage anecdote express Diocletian’s choice after retirement?

**A:** Asked by **Maximian, his former imperial colleague** to resume power, Diocletian reportedly preferred the **cabbages he had grown at Salona**: the enjoyment of private happiness over renewed ambition.

**Gibbon:** “He rejected the temptation with a smile of pity, calmly observing that if he could show Maximian the cabbages which he had planted with his own hands at Salona, he should no longer be urged to relinquish the enjoyment of happiness for the pursuit of power.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1452](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1452).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 35. Diocletian — a ruler’s blindness

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-court-truth -->
<a id="gibbon-03-court-truth"></a>

**Q:** What danger of court government does Gibbon illustrate through Diocletian’s reflections in retirement?

**A:** Ministers can **control everything the sovereign learns**, turning exalted power into dependence on those who filter the truth.

**Gibbon:** “Secluded from mankind by his exalted dignity, the truth is concealed from his knowledge; he can see only with their eyes, he hears nothing but their misrepresentations.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1452](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1452).

## The struggle for sole power

### 36. The Tetrarchy — harmony without a successor

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-tetrarchy-failure -->
<a id="gibbon-03-tetrarchy-failure"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon think Diocletian’s balance of power failed after his retirement?

**A:** Its harmony depended on **Diocletian’s personal authority** and an unlikely absence of rivalry among four rulers. The institutional arrangement could not reproduce the man who held it together.

**Gibbon:** “It required such a fortunate mixture of different tempers and abilities as could scarcely be found or even expected a second time; two emperors without jealousy, two Caesars without ambition, and the same general interest invariably pursued by four independent princes.”

**More:** In **306**, the soldiers acclaimed **Constantine**, son of Constantius I, while **Maxentius**, son of Maximian, seized power at Rome. Dynastic claims and military backing disrupted the planned succession.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1471](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1471); [Gibbon, Ch. XIV, L1483](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1483); [Gibbon, Ch. XIV, L1487](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1487).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 37. Constantine — proclaimed at York

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-constantine-accession -->
<a id="gibbon-03-constantine-accession"></a>

**Q:** How did Constantine gain power in 306?

**A:** After **Constantius I died at York**, his troops proclaimed his son **Constantine** emperor. Galerius accepted his government of the western provinces, but recognised him only as **Caesar**, promoting Severus II to Augustus.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1483](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1483).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 38. Maxentius — Rome’s revolt

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-maxentius-accession -->
<a id="gibbon-03-maxentius-accession"></a>

**Q:** Why did Rome support Maxentius in 306?

**A:** Resentment of **taxation and the capital’s loss of status**, joined to the Praetorians’ fear for their position, brought **Maxentius, Maximian’s son**, to power. Maximian returned from retirement to support him.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1485](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1485); [Gibbon, L1487](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1487).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 39. Severus II — abandoned by his army

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-severus-end -->
<a id="gibbon-03-severus-end"></a>

**Q:** How did Severus II lose the throne in 307?

**A:** Sent against **Maxentius, the ruler proclaimed at Rome**, he lost troops to his opponents, withdrew to Ravenna, and surrendered to **Maximian, Maxentius’s father and a former emperor** on a promise of safety. He was nevertheless put to death.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1489](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1489).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 40. Maximian — the failed return

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-maximian-end -->
<a id="gibbon-03-maximian-end"></a>

**Q:** How did Maximian’s repeated return to power end?

**A:** After trying to usurp the position of his son-in-law **Constantine** in **310**, Maximian was captured and compelled to die. His second career ended in civil strife, not a recovery of his former authority.

**Gibbon:** “He deserved his fate; but we should find more reason to applaud the humanity of Constantine if he had spared an old man, the benefactor of his father, and the father of his wife.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1499](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1499).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 41. Galerius — a divided inheritance

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-galerius-end -->
<a id="gibbon-03-galerius-end"></a>

**Q:** When did Galerius die, and what became of his territories?

**A:** He died after a **painful illness in 311**. **Licinius** took his European territories, and **Maximin Daia** his Asian territories.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1501](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1501).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 42. Constantine — the Milvian Bridge

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-milvian -->
<a id="gibbon-03-milvian"></a>

**Q:** What did Constantine’s victory at the Milvian Bridge achieve?

**A:** On **28 October 312**, he defeated **Maxentius**, who drowned in the Tiber during the retreat. Constantine became master of Italy and Africa.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1513](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1513).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 43. Constantine — the end of the Praetorians

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-praetorians-end -->
<a id="gibbon-03-praetorians-end"></a>

**Q:** What became of the Praetorian Guard after Constantine defeated Maxentius?

**A:** Constantine **abolished the Guard in 312**, destroyed its fortified camp, and dispersed survivors to frontier units. The force that had repeatedly made emperors lost its base at Rome.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1517](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1517).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 44. Maximin Daia — the East passes to Licinius

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-maximin-end -->
<a id="gibbon-03-maximin-end"></a>

**Q:** How did Licinius gain sole control of the East in 313?

**A:** He **defeated Maximin Daia**, who withdrew and died at Tarsus later that year.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1519](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1519).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 45. Constantine — one emperor again

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-constantine-sole -->
<a id="gibbon-03-constantine-sole"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Constantine become sole emperor?

**A:** In **324**, after defeating **Licinius**, decisively at **Chrysopolis**. Licinius surrendered; Constantine later had him executed despite a promise to spare him.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1533](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1533); [Gibbon, L1537](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1537); [DIR: Licinius](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/licinius.htm).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

## Christianity: Gibbon’s explanation

### 46. Religion — the historian’s task

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-historian-theologian -->
<a id="gibbon-03-historian-theologian"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon distinguish the historian’s treatment of religion from the theologian’s?

**A:** The theologian describes religion’s **divine purity**; the historian examines its **human history**, including the errors and corruption acquired among its followers.

**Gibbon:** “The theologian may indulge the pleasing task of describing Religion as she descended from Heaven, arrayed in her native purity. A more melancholy duty is imposed on the historian. He must discover the inevitable mixture of error and corruption which she contracted in a long residence upon earth, among a weak and degenerate race of beings.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1556](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1556).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 47. Christianity — Gibbon’s five causes

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-five-causes -->
<a id="gibbon-03-five-causes"></a>

**Q:** What five human causes does Gibbon give for Christianity’s growth?

**A:** **Exclusive zeal; belief in a future life; claimed miracles; austere morals; and church organisation.** They explain, in his argument, how a small body gained converts, endurance, and coordinated strength.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1558](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1558).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 48. Christianity — a universal invitation

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-universal-mission -->
<a id="gibbon-03-universal-mission"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon explain Christianity’s combination of exclusive belief and universal appeal?

**A:** It retained devotion to **one true God** while opening membership beyond a single people. Converts were urged to spread their faith, and Gentiles could enter without accepting the full Mosaic ceremonial law.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1568](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1568).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 49. Christianity — religion in ordinary life

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-idolatry-daily -->
<a id="gibbon-03-idolatry-daily"></a>

**Q:** Why was refusing idolatry socially costly for early Christians in Gibbon’s account?

**A:** Traditional worship permeated **work, public office, festivals, weddings, funerals, and entertainment**. Refusing its rites could mean withdrawing from everyday relationships, not merely avoiding temples.

**Gibbon:** “The innumerable deities and rites of Polytheism were closely interwoven with every circumstance of business or pleasure, of public or of private life;”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1582](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1582).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 50. Christianity — eternity near at hand

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-afterlife -->
<a id="gibbon-03-afterlife"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon connect belief in a future life with early Christian resolve?

**A:** The promised afterlife made present loss less terrifying; expectation of an **imminent judgment** intensified both moral discipline and courage.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1592](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1592).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 51. Miracles — belief as a historical force

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-miracles-conversion -->
<a id="gibbon-03-miracles-conversion"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon count belief in miracles among the causes of Christianity’s growth?

**A:** Reported **healings, exorcisms, visions, and resurrections** reassured believers and offered prospective converts signs of divine power. The belief could strengthen the church’s authority whether or not the historian accepted the miracle.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XV, L1598](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1598).

### 52. Miracles — a challenge left unanswered

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-miracle-skepticism -->
<a id="gibbon-03-miracle-skepticism"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon use the story of Theophilus, bishop of Antioch, to question reported resurrections?

**A:** A pagan offered to convert if shown **one person raised from the dead**. Gibbon pointedly observes that Theophilus declined the challenge, contrasting the abundance of reported miracles with the lack of a demonstrated example.

**Gibbon:** “It is somewhat remarkable that the prelate of the first Eastern church, however anxious for the conversion of his friend, thought proper to decline this fair and reasonable challenge.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1598](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1598).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 53. Christianity — the discipline of a minority

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-morals-reputation -->
<a id="gibbon-03-morals-reputation"></a>

**Q:** Which two human motives does Gibbon give for early Christian moral discipline?

**A:** **Repentance for past conduct** and **care for the community’s reputation**. Under hostile observation, each member’s behaviour could honour or disgrace the whole group.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1602](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1602); [Gibbon, L1606](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1606).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 54. The church — a federative republic

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-synods -->
<a id="gibbon-03-synods"></a>

**Q:** How did provincial synods strengthen the early church in Gibbon’s account?

**A:** Bishops of separate congregations met in **councils**, settled questions of belief and discipline, and communicated their decisions. Local churches acquired the strength of an organised federation.

**Gibbon:** “the catholic church soon assumed the form, and acquired the strength, of a great federative republic.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1620](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1620).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 55. The church — the patrimony of the poor

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-charity -->
<a id="gibbon-03-charity"></a>

**Q:** How did organised charity contribute to Christianity’s growth in Gibbon’s explanation?

**A:** Common funds supported **widows, orphans, the sick, the aged, travellers, and captives**. Practical care bound congregations together and offered protection to people neglected elsewhere.

**Gibbon:** “A generous intercourse of charity united the most distant provinces, and the smaller congregations were cheerfully assisted by the alms of their more opulent brethren.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1630](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1630).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 56. The church — punishment without an army

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-excommunication -->
<a id="gibbon-03-excommunication"></a>

**Q:** Why was excommunication an effective sanction in the early church?

**A:** It threatened **religious communion, material assistance, social ties, and hope of salvation** at once. Bishops could enforce discipline without possessing state power.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1632](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1632).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 57. Rome — roads for another conquest

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-roman-roads -->
<a id="gibbon-03-roman-roads"></a>

**Q:** How did the Roman empire help Christianity spread?

**A:** Its connected territories and **roads** let missionaries travel between cities and provinces. Infrastructure built for Roman power also carried a faith that would transform it.

**Gibbon:** “The public highways, which had been constructed for the use of the legions, opened an easy passage for the Christian missionaries from Damascus to Corinth, and from Italy to the extremity of Spain or Britain;”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1640](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1640).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

## Persecution and toleration

### 58. Persecution — inherited worship and chosen belief

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-ancestral-religion -->
<a id="gibbon-03-ancestral-religion"></a>

**Q:** Why, in Gibbon’s explanation, could Romans tolerate Judaism yet persecute Christianity?

**A:** They respected the claim of an **ancestral national religion**, while viewing conversion to Christianity as abandonment of inherited civic and religious duties. Christian refusal to worship other gods looked like disloyalty.

**Gibbon:** “The Jews were a nation; the Christians were a sect: and if it was natural for every community to respect the sacred institutions of their neighbors, it was incumbent on them to persevere in those of their ancestors.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1683](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1683).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 59. Nero — the Christians as scapegoats

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-nero-fire -->
<a id="gibbon-03-nero-fire"></a>

**Q:** Why did Nero persecute Christians at Rome in 64?

**A:** To divert suspicion that **he had caused the Great Fire**. Gibbon follows **the historian Tacitus** in describing Christians as scapegoats subjected to spectacular cruelty.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1699](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1699).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 60. Trajan — punish, but do not search

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-trajan-pliny -->
<a id="gibbon-03-trajan-pliny"></a>

**Q:** What policy did Trajan give Pliny, governor of Bithynia, for dealing with Christians around 112?

**A:** **Do not hunt them out or accept anonymous accusations**; punish those properly accused who persisted, but pardon those who renounced Christianity and worshipped the gods.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1709](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1709); [Gibbon, L1713](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1713).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 61. Persecution — torture for a denial

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-torture-denial -->
<a id="gibbon-03-torture-denial"></a>

**Q:** What inversion of normal judicial practice does Gibbon note in the treatment of Christians?

**A:** Torture was used to obtain **denial of the faith**, not confession of the alleged offence. A prisoner who performed sacrifice could escape punishment.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1713](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1713); [Gibbon, Ch. XVI, L1713](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1713).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 62. Decius — faith put to a public test

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-decius-sacrifice -->
<a id="gibbon-03-decius-sacrifice"></a>

**Q:** How did Decius’s order to sacrifice in 250 change the pressure on Christians?

**A:** A broad demand for **public sacrifice to the gods**, with certificates attesting compliance, made refusal visible. Christians faced a choice between religious fidelity and obedience to the state.

**More:** Surviving Egyptian certificates record sacrifice, libation, and tasting the offerings before officials.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XVI, L1735](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1735); [University of Michigan: certificate of sacrifice, AD 250](https://apps.lib.umich.edu/files/collections/papyrus/exhibits/papyri_james/1_2.html).

### 63. Cyprian — the bishop’s martyrdom

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-cyprian -->
<a id="gibbon-03-cyprian"></a>

**Q:** Who was Cyprian, and how did his life end?

**A:** The **bishop of Carthage**, an influential organiser and defender of episcopal authority. He was **beheaded in 258**, during Valerian’s persecution, after refusing to sacrifice.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1721](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1721); [Gibbon, L1723](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1723).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 64. Gallienus — peace for the church

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-gallienus-toleration -->
<a id="gibbon-03-gallienus-toleration"></a>

**Q:** What change did Gallienus bring to the treatment of Christians after Valerian?

**A:** He **ended his father’s persecution** and recognised Christian worship, opening a long interval of relative peace before the Great Persecution began in 303.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1737](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1737).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 65. Diocletian — the Great Persecution

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-great-persecution -->
<a id="gibbon-03-great-persecution"></a>

**Q:** When did the Great Persecution begin, and what did its first measures target?

**A:** In **303**. They targeted **church buildings, scriptures, church property, and Christians’ legal standing**; later edicts demanded sacrifice and intensified coercion.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1747](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1747); [Gibbon, L1751](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1751).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 66. Persecution — an uneven empire

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-uneven-persecution -->
<a id="gibbon-03-uneven-persecution"></a>

**Q:** How did the Great Persecution differ between the western and eastern provinces?

**A:** Its severity depended on the ruler. **Constantius I limited persecution in the far West**; **Galerius and Maximin Daia** pursued it far more vigorously in the East.

**More:** Gibbon notes that Constantius I allowed the destruction of churches while protecting Christians themselves.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1755](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1755).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 67. Galerius — the persecutor asks for prayers

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-galerius-toleration -->
<a id="gibbon-03-galerius-toleration"></a>

**Q:** What reversal did Galerius make in 311?

**A:** His **edict of toleration** allowed Christians to worship and assemble again, provided they respected public order. The dying persecutor asked them to pray for the emperors and the state.

**Gibbon:** “the most violent efforts of despotism are insufficient to extirpate a whole people, or to subdue their religious prejudices.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1761](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1761).

<!-- Tags: batch::03 -->

### 68. Gibbon — what should history believe?

<!-- ID: gibbon-03-evidence -->
<a id="gibbon-03-evidence"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon refuse simply to reproduce the accumulated stories of Christian martyrdom?

**A:** He insists on **testing the sources before repeating their stories**, especially when an author selects events to glorify a cause.

**Gibbon:** “But I cannot determine what I ought to transcribe till I am satisfied how much I ought to believe.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, L1763](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1763).
