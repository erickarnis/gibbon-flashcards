# Gibbon — Time and place

A companion to the cards for **Volumes I–II, Chapters I–XXVI**. Dates are AD. The narrative ends with the Gothic settlement of **382**, while the Roman empire is still functioning.

## The main sequence

| Period | Political movement | What to remember |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **96–180** | Nerva → Trajan → Hadrian → Antoninus Pius → Marcus Aurelius | Gibbon’s prosperous baseline; good rulers wield power that no institution can reliably restrain. Lucius Verus shares Marcus’s throne in 161–169. |
| **180–235** | Commodus → the struggle of 193 → the Severan dynasty | Soldiers increasingly decide who rules and what government must pay. Septimius Severus’s apparent success deepens this dependence. |
| **235–270** | Repeated military revolts, invasion, and regional breakaways | Valerian is captured in 260; Gallienus struggles with multiple fronts. Postumus establishes a western imperial government; Palmyra becomes the eastern power later ruled by Zenobia. |
| **270–284** | Aurelian and the soldier-emperors restore imperial strength | Aurelian recovers the eastern territories in 272 and the Gallic empire in 274; repeated assassinations still interrupt recovery. |
| **284–324** | Diocletian → Tetrarchy → renewed civil wars → Constantine | Four rulers share responsibility from 293. The abdications of 305 do not secure the next succession. Constantine wins sole power in 324. |
| **324–361** | Constantine → three sons → Constantius II → Julian | Constantinople is dedicated in 330. Constantine dies in 337; Constantius becomes sole ruler in 353. Julian advances from Caesar in 355 to sole emperor in 361. |
| **361–364** | Julian → Jovian → Valentinian I and Valens | Julian’s Persian expedition ends in his death in 363; Jovian buys the army’s escape. In 364 the brothers Valentinian and Valens divide responsibility for West and East. |
| **364–382** | Frontier pressure → Adrianople → Theodosius’s recovery | Valentinian dies in 375; Valens falls at Adrianople in 378. Gratian appoints Theodosius in 379. Settlement by 382 brings peace and continuing dependence on Gothic soldiers. |

Arrows indicate the main narrative, not a complete list of sole rulers. Joint emperors and rival governments often overlap.

## A schematic regional map

Read west to east. This is a spatial guide, not a map of fixed borders.

| | Western provinces | Central provinces | Eastern provinces |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Northern approaches** | Britain; Rhine frontier opposite German peoples | Alps, Danube, and Balkans | Black Sea; Armenia and the Caucasus |
| **Mediterranean world** | Spain and Gaul | Italy and Rome; Greece | Constantinople and Asia Minor; Syria and Palmyra |
| **Southern shore** | Mauretania | Roman Africa and Carthage | Egypt and Alexandria |
| **Main pressures in this narrative** | Franks across the lower Rhine; Gallic breakaway government | Alemanni into Italy; Goths across the Danube | Gothic sea raids; Persian power beyond the eastern frontier |

**Three geographical connections carry much of the story:**

- **Rhine–Danube:** the northern defensive system. Dacia projects beyond the Danube; Aurelian’s withdrawal makes the river a stronger frontier.
- **Bosporus:** Constantinople stands at the passage between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, between Europe and Asia, nearer both the Danubian and Persian theatres than Rome.
- **Mesopotamia:** the land between the Euphrates and Tigris. Ctesiphon, the Persian capital, lies on the Tigris. Armenia and the fortresses of northern Mesopotamia repeatedly shape Rome’s eastern wars.

Sources: [the frontier pressures](02-commodus-to-gallienus.md#gibbon-02-frontiers), [Constantinople](04-constantine-to-adrianople.md#gibbon-04-capital), [Trajan in the East](01-opening-emperors.md#gibbon-01-trajan-east), [Aurelian’s withdrawal](03-restoration-and-christianity.md#gibbon-03-aurelian-dacia), and [Gibbon’s account of the empire’s extent, Chapter I](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L584).

## Similar names, different people

| Name | Identity |
| --- | --- |
| **Septimius Severus** | Emperor 193–211; father of Caracalla and Geta. |
| **Alexander Severus** | Emperor 222–235; son of Julia Mamaea. |
| **Severus II** | Western Caesar from 305, Augustus from 306; defeated in the struggle with Maxentius and Maximian. |
| **Maximian** | Diocletian’s senior imperial colleague and father of Maxentius. |
| **Maximinus Thrax** | Soldier-emperor 235–238. |
| **Maximin Daia** | Eastern Caesar from 305; rival defeated by Licinius in 313. |
| **Constantius I** | Constantine’s father; Caesar under Diocletian, Augustus 305–306. |
| **Constantine II** | Constantine’s eldest surviving son at the succession of 337; killed invading Constans’s territory in 340. |
| **Constantius II** | Another son of Constantine; eventually sole emperor, 353–361. |
| **Eusebius of Caesarea** | Bishop and biographer of Constantine. |
| **The chamberlain Eusebius** | Powerful palace official under Constantius II. |
| **The elder Theodosius** | General active in Britain and Africa; father of Theodosius I. |
| **Theodosius I** | Appointed eastern emperor in 379 after Adrianople. |

Each card supplies the identification it needs; this table is a reference, not required preparation for a shuffled card.
