# Gibbon — Commodus to Gallienus

Chapters IV–X, with Gallienus’s death from XI. All dates are AD.

## Commodus

### 1. Commodus — inherited security

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-commodus-accession -->
<a id="gibbon-02-commodus-accession"></a>

**Q:** When did Commodus become sole emperor, and what made his accession unusually secure?

**A:** **180**, on the death of his father, **Marcus Aurelius**; he had been co-emperor since **177**. He inherited the allegiance of Senate and armies, without a rival to remove.

**Gibbon:** “the happy youth saw round him neither competitor to remove nor enemies to punish.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L761](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L761); [Chronology notes](sources/editorial-notes.md#chronology).

### 2. Commodus — weakness becoming cruelty

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-commodus-character -->
<a id="gibbon-02-commodus-character"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon explain the growth of Commodus’s cruelty?

**A:** A weak, fearful character surrendered to corrupt attendants; cruelty first obeyed others, then became a habit and finally a ruling passion.

**Gibbon:** “His cruelty, which at first obeyed the dictates of others, degenerated into habit, and at length became the ruling passion of his soul.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L763](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L763).

### 3. Commodus — leaving the frontier

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-commodus-danube -->
<a id="gibbon-02-commodus-danube"></a>

**Q:** What did Commodus do about Marcus’s unfinished Danubian war?

**A:** He **made peace with the Quadi and Marcomanni and returned to Rome**. Gibbon makes the contrast between the hardships of camp and the pleasures of the capital central to his decision.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L765](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L765).

### 4. Commodus — the assassin’s announcement

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-commodus-lucilla -->
<a id="gibbon-02-commodus-lucilla"></a>

**Q:** In Gibbon’s account, what failed assassination turned Commodus against the Senate?

**A:** The conspiracy of **Lucilla, his sister and the widow of Lucius Verus**. Her assassin announced, “The senate sends you this”; the warning allowed the guards to seize him, but the words fixed a lasting suspicion in Commodus’s mind.

**More:** The conspiracy arose within the palace, rather than from a collective decision of the Senate.

**Gibbon:** “The menace prevented the deed; the assassin was seized by the guards and immediately revealed the authors of the conspiracy.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L769](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L769).

### 5. Commodus — distinction becomes a crime

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-commodus-informers -->
<a id="gibbon-02-commodus-informers"></a>

**Q:** Why did distinction become dangerous under Commodus, in Gibbon’s account?

**A:** Wealth tempted confiscation; virtue implied criticism; distinguished service suggested a rival. **Informers** converted those advantages into accusations of treason.

**Gibbon:** “Suspicion was equivalent to proof; trial to condemnation.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L771](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L771).

### 6. Commodus — petitioners in arms

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-commodus-perennis -->
<a id="gibbon-02-commodus-perennis"></a>

**Q:** What made the fall of Commodus’s minister Perennis an omen of military disorder?

**A:** In **185**, a deputation of **1,500 soldiers from Britain**, in Gibbon’s account, marched to Rome and obtained the minister’s death. A distant army had learned that it could dictate to the emperor.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L773](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L773).

### 7. Commodus — the Roman Hercules

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-commodus-hercules -->
<a id="gibbon-02-commodus-hercules"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon ridicule Commodus’s claim to be a new Hercules?

**A:** Hercules’s legendary destruction of dangerous beasts benefited an unsettled world. Commodus imported animals to a secure capital so that he could kill them in public, turning a useful labour into costly vanity.

**Gibbon:** “an enterprise equally ridiculous for the prince and oppressive for the people.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L781](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L781).

### 8. Commodus — victories that could not be lost

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-commodus-gladiator -->
<a id="gibbon-02-commodus-gladiator"></a>

**Q:** What disgraced Commodus’s appearances as a gladiator?

**A:** The emperor entered a profession Romans considered dishonourable, faced opponents unable safely to defeat him, and charged the public an enormous fee.

**More:** Gibbon records **735 appearances** as a **secutor**, a gladiator armed with a sword and shield.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L783](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L783).

### 9. Commodus — the danger nearest home

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-commodus-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-commodus-death"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Commodus die, and who followed him?

**A:** On **31 December 192**, palace conspirators poisoned him; a wrestler then **strangled** him. **Pertinax** succeeded at the new year. Marcia, his concubine, and senior household officers acted because they feared for their own lives.

**Gibbon:** “He had shed with impunity the noblest blood of Rome: he perished as soon as he was dreaded by his own domestics.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L785](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L785); [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L787](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L787); [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L791](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L791).

## Pertinax and the sale of the empire


### 10. Pertinax — an unexpected summons

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-pertinax-accession -->
<a id="gibbon-02-pertinax-accession"></a>

**Q:** How did Pertinax become emperor in 193?

**A:** Commodus’s killers chose the respected **city prefect and former servant of Marcus**. The Praetorians accepted him, and the Senate ratified his elevation. Awakened at night, he initially expected execution.

**Gibbon:** “Instead of death, they offered him the throne of the Roman world.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L787](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L787); [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L789](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L789).


### 11. Pertinax — a poor republic

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-pertinax-reform -->
<a id="gibbon-02-pertinax-reform"></a>

**Q:** What ideal of government does Gibbon illustrate through Pertinax’s reforms?

**A:** **Justice and restraint before wealth**: he would govern a poor state honestly rather than fill its treasury by oppression.

**More:** He recalled innocent exiles, restored confiscated property, restrained informers, and removed oppressive taxes.

**Gibbon:** “he was better satisfied to administer a poor republic with innocence than to acquire riches by the ways of tyranny and dishonor.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L797](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L797); [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L795](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L795).


### 12. Pertinax — virtue without protection

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-pertinax-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-pertinax-death"></a>

**Q:** Why and when did the Praetorians murder Pertinax?

**A:** In **193**, after a reign of about three months. They resented his efforts to restore discipline and end the licence of Commodus’s reign; he confronted the mutineers in person and was cut down. **Didius Julianus** followed.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L801](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L801); [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L799](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L799); [Gibbon, Ch. V, L826](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L826).


### 13. Didius Julianus — the empire at auction

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-julianus-auction -->
<a id="gibbon-02-julianus-auction"></a>

**Q:** How did Didius Julianus obtain the throne in 193?

**A:** He **outbid Sulpicianus, Pertinax’s father-in-law**, for the support of the Praetorians. The guards proclaimed the higher bidder emperor and compelled senatorial recognition.

**More:** Gibbon gives the winning offer as 6,250 drachmas to each soldier, against his rival’s 5,000.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. V, L826](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L826); [Gibbon, Ch. V, L828](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L828).


### 14. Didius Julianus — a purchased throne

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-julianus-isolation -->
<a id="gibbon-02-julianus-isolation"></a>

**Q:** Why did buying the throne fail to give Julianus secure power?

**A:** His purchase won neither public allegiance nor the frontier armies. **Septimius Severus in Pannonia, Niger in Syria, and Albinus in Britain** became rival claimants.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. V, L832](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L832).

### 15. Didius Julianus — sixty-six days

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-julianus-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-julianus-death"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Julianus’s reign end?

**A:** In **193**, as **Septimius Severus** approached Rome. The guards abandoned Julianus; the Senate deposed him and sentenced him to death. He was **beheaded**, after only **66 days** in power.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. V, L844](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L844).

## Septimius Severus

### 16. Severus — the road from the Danube

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-severus-accession -->
<a id="gibbon-02-severus-accession"></a>

**Q:** What gave Septimius Severus the advantage in the struggle for Rome in 193?

**A:** His **Pannonian army** acclaimed him, and its proximity to Italy allowed a rapid march across the Alps before distant rivals could intervene. He presented himself as the avenger of Pertinax.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. V, L836](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L836); [Gibbon, Ch. V, L834](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L834).


### 17. Severus — disarming the guard

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-severus-guards -->
<a id="gibbon-02-severus-guards"></a>

**Q:** How did Septimius Severus dispose of the Praetorians who had betrayed Pertinax?

**A:** He ordered them to meet him **unarmed in ceremonial dress**, surrounded them with his own troops, dismissed and stripped them, and banished them from the vicinity of Rome.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. V, L846](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L846).

### 18. Severus — mastering East and West

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-severus-rivals -->
<a id="gibbon-02-severus-rivals"></a>

**Q:** Which two major rivals did Septimius Severus defeat after taking Rome?

**A:** **Pescennius Niger in the East, in 194**, then **Clodius Albinus in the West, in 197**. Their defeat made his possession of the empire secure.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. V, L852](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L852).

### 19. Severus — the empire as an estate

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-severus-property -->
<a id="gibbon-02-severus-property"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon explain the coexistence of Septimius Severus’s despotism and effective government?

**A:** Once secure, he protected and improved the empire as an owner tends valuable property. Firm justice and prosperity could serve a despot’s interest without requiring humanity.

**Gibbon:** “Severus considered the Roman empire as his property, and had no sooner secured the possession than he bestowed his care on the cultivation and improvement of so valuable an acquisition.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. V, L858](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L858).

### 20. Severus — the hidden cost of peace

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-severus-army -->
<a id="gibbon-02-severus-army"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon see Septimius Severus’s apparently successful reign as a turning point in decline?

**A:** He strengthened **military privilege and expectations of pay and gifts**, while weakening the Senate’s remaining authority. Immediate order concealed a lasting dependence on the army.

**Gibbon:** “Although the wounds of civil war appeared completely healed, its mortal poison still lurked in the vitals of the constitution.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. V, L860](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L860); [Gibbon, Ch. V, L864](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L864).


### 21. Severus — the disappointment of greatness

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-severus-satisfaction -->
<a id="gibbon-02-severus-satisfaction"></a>

**Q:** What disappointment does Gibbon find at the summit of Septimius Severus’s ambition?

**A:** Acquiring power exercised his abilities; possessing it brought anxiety, age, and satiety. The remaining ambition was to perpetuate his family’s rule.

**Gibbon:** “The ascent to greatness, however steep and dangerous, may entertain an active spirit with the consciousness and exercise of its own powers: but the possession of a throne could never yet afford a lasting satisfaction to an ambitious mind.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L883](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L883).

### 22. Severus — a divided inheritance

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-severus-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-severus-death"></a>

**Q:** When and where did Septimius Severus die, and who inherited his power?

**A:** He **died after illness at York in 211**, leaving his sons **Caracalla and Geta** as joint rulers. His appeals for brotherly concord could not overcome their hostility.

**Gibbon:** “In his last moments he recommended concord to his sons, and his sons to the army.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L891](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L891).

## The later Severans

### 23. Geta — reconciliation as a trap

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-geta-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-geta-death"></a>

**Q:** How did Caracalla become sole emperor?

**A:** He had his brother and co-emperor **Geta murdered in late 211**, during a supposed reconciliation in their mother **Julia Domna’s** apartment. He then secured the soldiers with a lavish gift.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L897](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L897); [DIR: Geta](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/geta.htm).

### 24. Caracalla — universal citizenship

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-caracalla-citizenship -->
<a id="gibbon-02-caracalla-citizenship"></a>

**Q:** What did Caracalla’s citizenship edict do, and how does Gibbon interpret its motive?

**A:** In **212**, it extended Roman citizenship to **nearly all free inhabitants of the empire**. Gibbon interprets the grant principally as a way to enlarge the tax base, including liability for inheritance taxes.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L943](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L943); [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L957](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L957); [Myles Lavan: The Spread of Roman Citizenship, 14–212 CE](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/10023/12646/1/Lavan_2016_PandP_SpreadRomanCitizenship_RevisedAAM.pdf).


### 25. Caracalla — one dangerous maxim

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-caracalla-army -->
<a id="gibbon-02-caracalla-army"></a>

**Q:** What political maxim did Caracalla retain from his father, Septimius Severus, in Gibbon’s account?

**A:** **Secure the army’s affection and treat the rest of the subjects as secondary.** Greater pay and gifts purchased military support at the expense of the empire.

**Gibbon:** “To secure the affections of the army, and to esteem the rest of his subjects as of little moment.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L903](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L903).

### 26. Caracalla — the dangerous prediction

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-caracalla-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-caracalla-death"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Caracalla die, and who succeeded him?

**A:** He was **stabbed in 217**, on the road from Edessa to Carrhae. **Macrinus**, his Praetorian prefect, organised the plot and became emperor. In Gibbon’s account, a prediction that Macrinus would reign made delay more dangerous than conspiracy.

**Gibbon:** “Macrinus read his fate, and resolved to prevent it.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L905](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L905).

### 27. Macrinus — two rates of pay

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-macrinus-pay -->
<a id="gibbon-02-macrinus-pay"></a>

**Q:** Why did Macrinus’s cautious attempt to reduce military costs fail?

**A:** He preserved existing soldiers’ benefits but enrolled **new recruits on lower terms**. Recruits resented the inequality, while veterans feared their own turn would come; the concentrated army in Syria could combine its grievances.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L909](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L909).

### 28. Elagabalus — a manufactured inheritance

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-elagabalus-accession -->
<a id="gibbon-02-elagabalus-accession"></a>

**Q:** How did Julia Maesa, Elagabalus’s grandmother, help him overthrow Macrinus in 218?

**A:** She used her wealth and promoted the claim that her grandson, a **young priest at Emesa**, was Caracalla’s son. Discontented soldiers rallied to him; Macrinus fled after defeat and was killed.

**More:** Maesa was the sister of Julia Domna, Caracalla’s mother.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L911](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L911); [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L913](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L913).


### 29. Elagabalus — the god from Emesa

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-elagabalus-stone -->
<a id="gibbon-02-elagabalus-stone"></a>

**Q:** What religious project dominated Elagabalus’s reign?

**A:** He sought to elevate **the sun god of Emesa**, represented by a **black conical stone**, above Rome’s other gods. The sacred stone was brought to Rome and installed in a splendid Palatine temple.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L919](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L919).

### 30. Elagabalus — the adopted rival

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-elagabalus-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-elagabalus-death"></a>

**Q:** How did the adoption of Alexander Severus as heir contribute to Elagabalus’s fall?

**A:** **Alexander, Elagabalus’s cousin and adopted heir**, gained the troops’ affection; Elagabalus grew jealous and endangered him. In **222**, the Praetorians murdered **Elagabalus and his mother**, and Alexander became emperor.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L927](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L927); [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L925](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L925).


### 31. Alexander Severus — power behind the throne

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-alexander-mamaea -->
<a id="gibbon-02-alexander-mamaea"></a>

**Q:** Who shaped the young Alexander Severus’s government?

**A:** His mother **Julia Mamaea**, assisted by senatorial advisers and the jurist **Ulpian**. Gibbon credits their administration with restoring lawful government after Elagabalus.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L931](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L931).

### 32. Alexander Severus — the purple cannot protect

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-alexander-ulpian -->
<a id="gibbon-02-alexander-ulpian"></a>

**Q:** What did the murder of Ulpian, the Praetorian prefect, reveal about Alexander Severus’s power?

**A:** The emperor could not protect his own **Praetorian prefect and legal adviser** from the soldiers. Ulpian was killed in the palace despite Alexander’s attempt to shelter him.

**Gibbon:** “He was pursued into the Imperial palace and massacred at the feet of his master, who vainly strove to cover him with the purple and to obtain his pardon from the inexorable soldiers.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VI, L939](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L939).

### 33. Alexander Severus — the army chooses again

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-alexander-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-alexander-death"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Alexander Severus’s reign end, and who replaced him?

**A:** In **235**, mutinous soldiers proclaimed their commander **Maximinus Thrax** and murdered **Alexander and his mother, Julia Mamaea**. The army again claimed the power to confer the throne.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L990](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L990); [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L988](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L988).

## The crisis of 235–249


### 34. Succession — the useful prejudice

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-succession-rule -->
<a id="gibbon-02-succession-rule"></a>

**Q:** How can Gibbon praise adoption for merit yet defend hereditary succession?

**A:** **Choosing a good ruler and securing a peaceful succession are different problems.** Adoption may select merit; an accepted hereditary rule can prevent competing claimants and armies from fighting over every vacancy.

**Gibbon:** “Satire and declamation may paint these obvious topics in the most dazzling colors, but our more serious thoughts will respect a useful prejudice that establishes a rule of succession independent of the passions of mankind;”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L978](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L978); [Gibbon, Ch. III, L722](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L722); [Gibbon, Ch. IV, L759](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L759).

### 35. Maximinus — the fear of contempt

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-maximinus-resentment -->
<a id="gibbon-02-maximinus-resentment"></a>

**Q:** What motive does Gibbon give for Maximinus’s cruelty?

**A:** **Fear of contempt for his humble origins.** People who had helped him and people who had slighted him were equally dangerous because they knew his earlier obscurity.

**Gibbon:** “But those who had spurned and those who had protected the Thracian were guilty of the same crime, the knowledge of his original obscurity.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L992](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L992).

### 36. The African Gordians — a forced rebellion

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-gordians-revolt -->
<a id="gibbon-02-gordians-revolt"></a>

**Q:** How did Gordian I and Gordian II become rival emperors?

**A:** In **238**, an African revolt against oppressive taxation elevated **Gordian I, the provincial governor, and his son Gordian II**. The Senate recognised them and declared Maximinus an enemy.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L998](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L998); [DIR: Gordian I](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/gordo1.htm); [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L1004](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1004).


### 37. The African Gordians — a brief reign

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-gordians-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-gordians-death"></a>

**Q:** How did the joint reign of Gordian I and Gordian II end?

**A:** In **238**, forces loyal to Maximinus defeated the African revolt. **Gordian II died in battle; Gordian I killed himself**. The Senate continued resistance under Pupienus and Balbinus.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L1010](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1010); [DIR: Gordian I](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/gordo1.htm); [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L1012](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1012).


### 38. Pupienus and Balbinus — the Senate’s choice

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-pupienus-balbinus -->
<a id="gibbon-02-pupienus-balbinus"></a>

**Q:** Why did the Senate choose Pupienus and Balbinus in 238?

**A:** To continue the struggle against **Maximinus** after the African Gordians died: **Pupienus** would lead the war, **Balbinus** the civil government. Popular pressure also secured the title of Caesar for the boy **Gordian III, grandson of Gordian I**.

**More:** Gibbon generally calls Pupienus **Maximus**. He is a different man from **Maximinus**, the emperor they opposed.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L1012](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1012); [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L1016](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1016).


### 39. Maximinus — defeated by a siege

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-maximinus-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-maximinus-death"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Maximinus fall?

**A:** In **238**, his own soldiers killed him and his son during the failed **siege of Aquileia**. Hunger, disease, and anger at his punishments dissolved their loyalty.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L1024](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1024).

### 40. Pupienus and Balbinus — a divided defence

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-pupienus-balbinus-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-pupienus-balbinus-death"></a>

**Q:** Why could Pupienus and Balbinus not protect themselves after Maximinus’s fall?

**A:** Their mutual suspicion prevented cooperation against the Praetorians. In **238**, the guards seized and murdered both; **Gordian III** became emperor.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L1028](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1028); [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L1030](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1030).


### 41. Gordian III — an able father-in-law

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-gordian-timesitheus -->
<a id="gibbon-02-gordian-timesitheus"></a>

**Q:** Who was Timesitheus, and why did he matter to Gordian III?

**A:** The emperor’s **father-in-law and Praetorian prefect**, an experienced administrator who helped direct government and the Persian campaign. He died in **243**, removing a major support of the young ruler.

**More:** Gibbon calls him **Misitheus**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L1034](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1034); [DIR: Gordian III](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/gordo3.htm).

### 42. Gordian III — an uncertain death

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-gordian-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-gordian-death"></a>

**Q:** When did Gordian III die, and who succeeded him?

**A:** He **died during the Persian campaign in 244**, under disputed circumstances. **Philip the Arab**, his Praetorian prefect, was acclaimed emperor by the army.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L1034](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1034); [DIR: Gordian III](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/gordo3.htm).

### 43. Philip — Rome’s thousandth year

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-philip-millennium -->
<a id="gibbon-02-philip-millennium"></a>

**Q:** What great anniversary did Philip celebrate in 248?

**A:** The **thousandth anniversary of Rome’s foundation**, with magnificent secular games. Gibbon sets their splendour against anxiety about the empire’s future.

**Gibbon:** “The devout were employed in the rites of superstition, whilst the reflecting few revolved in their anxious minds the past history and the future fate of the empire.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VII, L1038](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1038).

### 44. Philip — the commander sent to restore order

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-philip-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-philip-death"></a>

**Q:** How did Decius replace Philip?

**A:** Sent to restore discipline on the Danube, **Decius was proclaimed emperor by the troops** and advanced into Italy. **Philip died in 249**, in battle or shortly afterwards, and Decius secured the throne.

**Gibbon:** “The legions of Moesia forced their judge to become their accomplice.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1155](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1155).

## Persia and Germany

### 45. Persia — a renewed rival

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-ardashir -->
<a id="gibbon-02-ardashir"></a>

**Q:** Who founded the Sasanian monarchy, and what Roman claims did he challenge?

**A:** **Ardashir I**, Gibbon’s **Artaxerxes**, overthrew the Parthian ruling house in **224**. In Gibbon’s account, he demanded restoration of the ancient Persian dominions, including Roman territories in Asia.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VIII, L1087](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1087); [Encyclopaedia Iranica: Ardashir I](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ardasir-i/).

### 46. Persia — salvation through cultivation

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-persia-agriculture -->
<a id="gibbon-02-persia-agriculture"></a>

**Q:** What does Gibbon admire in the agricultural duties he attributes to Zoroastrian religion?

**A:** They turn piety towards **useful labour**: planting, irrigation, and cultivation rather than fasting and celibacy.

**Gibbon:** “The saint, in the Magian religion, is obliged to beget children, to plant useful trees, to destroy noxious animals, to convey water to the dry lands of Persia, and to work out his salvation by pursuing all the labors of agriculture.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VIII, L1075](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1075).

### 47. Ardashir — the foundation beneath the army

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-ardashir-justice -->
<a id="gibbon-02-ardashir-justice"></a>

**Q:** What links military power to justice in Gibbon’s maxim of Ardashir I, the Persian king he calls Artaxerxes?

**A:** **An army requires taxes; taxes depend on agriculture; agriculture requires justice and moderation.** Oppression ultimately destroys the foundation of power.

**Gibbon:** “The authority of the prince,” said Artaxerxes, “must be defended by a military force; that force can only be maintained by taxes; all taxes must, at last, fall upon agriculture; and agriculture can never flourish except under the protection of justice and moderation.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. VIII, L1091](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1091).

### 48. Germany — the fetters of possession

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-germany-poverty -->
<a id="gibbon-02-germany-poverty"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon connect German poverty with political independence?

**A:** A people with few possessions offers fewer means of coercion through wealth and dependence. He contrasts their independence with the richer empire’s subjection.

**Gibbon:** “Their poverty secured their freedom, since our desires and our possessions are the strongest fetters of despotism.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IX, L1114](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1114).

### 49. Germany — the chief and his companions

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-germany-companions -->
<a id="gibbon-02-germany-companions"></a>

**Q:** What personal bond does Gibbon describe in German warrior bands?

**A:** Chosen companions devoted themselves to a chief, defended him in battle, and received hospitality and gifts. Honour bound followers to his person more strongly than a magistrate’s commands.

**Gibbon:** “The chiefs combated for victory, the companions for the chief.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IX, L1122](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1122).

### 50. Germany — the immortality of song

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-germany-bards -->
<a id="gibbon-02-germany-bards"></a>

**Q:** Why does Gibbon give the bards a place in his account of German warfare?

**A:** Their songs made ancestral glory immediate at feasts and battles, strengthening the desire for fame and contempt of death.

**Gibbon:** “The immortality so vainly promised by the priests was in some degree conferred by the bards.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. IX, L1134](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1134).

## Decius to Gallienus

### 51. The third-century crisis — fragments and conjecture

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-history-fragments -->
<a id="gibbon-02-history-fragments"></a>

**Q:** What limit does Gibbon set himself when evidence for the third-century crisis is fragmentary?

**A:** He must collect, compare, and conjecture, but **must not give conjecture the status of fact**.

**Gibbon:** “Surrounded with imperfect fragments, always concise, often obscure, and sometimes contradictory, he is reduced to collect, to compare, and to conjecture”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1153](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1153).

### 52. Decius — the emperor in the marsh

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-decius-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-decius-death"></a>

**Q:** How did Decius’s reign end in 251?

**A:** He and his son **Herennius Etruscus, his co-emperor**, were **killed fighting the Goths under Cniva**, in the battle at **Abrittus**. Gibbon describes Roman troops trapped in a morass. **Trebonianus Gallus** succeeded.

**More:** Gibbon calls the site **Forum Terebronii**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1175](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1175); [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1181](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1181); [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1183](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1183); [DIR: Decius](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/decius.htm).


### 53. Gallus — buying a withdrawal

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-gallus-tribute -->
<a id="gibbon-02-gallus-tribute"></a>

**Q:** Why was Gallus’s peace with the Goths so humiliating to Roman opinion?

**A:** The invaders kept their **booty and captives**, and received a promise of **annual payment**. What Rome had once bestowed as generosity now looked like tribute exacted by a victor.

**Gibbon:** “the prince, who by a necessary concession had probably saved his country, became the object of the general contempt and aversion.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1185](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1185).

### 54. Gallus and Aemilianus — victory buys the army

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-gallus-aemilianus -->
<a id="gibbon-02-gallus-aemilianus"></a>

**Q:** How did Aemilianus replace Gallus in 253?

**A:** He defeated invaders on the Danube and **distributed the money intended for tribute to his soldiers**, who proclaimed him emperor. As he approached, Gallus’s own troops killed **Gallus and his son Volusianus**.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1187](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1187).

### 55. Aemilianus — a few months of power

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-aemilianus-valerian -->
<a id="gibbon-02-aemilianus-valerian"></a>

**Q:** How did Aemilianus lose the throne in 253?

**A:** His soldiers murdered him when **Valerian** approached with a stronger army. Valerian had been summoned to support Gallus, but arrived too late to save him and became emperor himself.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1189](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1189).

### 56. Valerian and Gallienus — joint and sole rule

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-valerian-gallienus -->
<a id="gibbon-02-valerian-gallienus"></a>

**Q:** When did Valerian and Gallienus rule together, and when did Gallienus rule alone?

**A:** **253–260 together**: Valerian raised his son Gallienus as a colleague. **260–268 alone**: Gallienus continued after Valerian’s capture by Persia.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1191](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1191).

### 57. The empire — pressure on several fronts

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-frontiers -->
<a id="gibbon-02-frontiers"></a>

**Q:** Where did the main foreign attacks strike the empire under Valerian and Gallienus?

**A:** **Franks** crossed the lower Rhine into Gaul; **Alemanni** threatened the upper Rhine and Italy; **Goths** attacked across the Danube and Black Sea; **Persians** pressed into Mesopotamia and Syria.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1191](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1191); [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1193](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1193); [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1199](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1199); [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1205](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1205); [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1217](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1217).

### 58. Athens — books and arms

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-athens-books -->
<a id="gibbon-02-athens-books"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon answer the story that the Goths spared Athenian libraries to keep the Greeks unwarlike?

**A:** He doubts the anecdote and rejects its reasoning: learning need not weaken military courage. Intellectual and military achievement can flourish together.

**Gibbon:** “In the most polite and powerful nations, genius of every kind has displayed itself about the same period; and the age of science has generally been the age of military virtue and success.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1215](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1215).

### 59. Valerian — a captive emperor

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-valerian-capture -->
<a id="gibbon-02-valerian-capture"></a>

**Q:** What disaster overtook Valerian in 260?

**A:** **Shapur I of Persia**, Gibbon’s **Sapor**, captured him near **Edessa**. Valerian never recovered his freedom and died in captivity; the precise circumstances of his death are uncertain.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1219](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1219); [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1225](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1225).


### 60. Valerian — the limits of a memorable story

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-valerian-footstool -->
<a id="gibbon-02-valerian-footstool"></a>

**Q:** How does Gibbon treat the story that Shapur used Valerian as a mounting block and preserved his stuffed skin?

**A:** As a striking moral tale whose **truth may reasonably be doubted**. Valerian’s captivity is secure; these humiliations are not.

**Gibbon:** “The voice of history, which is often little more than the organ of hatred or flattery, reproaches Sapor with a proud abuse of the rights of conquest.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1225](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1225).

### 61. Odenathus — Palmyra protects Rome

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-odenathus -->
<a id="gibbon-02-odenathus"></a>

**Q:** Who helped check Persia after Valerian’s capture?

**A:** **Odenathus of Palmyra**, who rallied forces and harassed Shapur’s retreat. Gibbon makes a provincial leader the defender of the imperial name.

**Gibbon:** “The majesty of Rome, oppressed by a Persian, was protected by a Syrian or Arab of Palmyra.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1223](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1223).

### 62. Gallienus — many talents, one omission

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-gallienus-portrait -->
<a id="gibbon-02-gallienus-portrait"></a>

**Q:** What is the point of Gibbon’s hostile portrait of Gallienus’s accomplishments?

**A:** A prince may possess many talents and neglect the one office that matters. Gibbon turns the contrast between private accomplishments and public failure into an epigram.

**Gibbon:** “He was a master of several curious but useless sciences, a ready orator, an elegant poet, a skillful gardener, an excellent cook, and most contemptible prince.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1227](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1227).

### 63. Gallienus — defending a fractured empire

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-gallienus-defense -->
<a id="gibbon-02-gallienus-defense"></a>

**Q:** What military response under Gallienus complicates Gibbon’s portrait of an idle emperor?

**A:** He **fought to defend the frontiers and strengthened mobile cavalry forces**, notably around Milan. Military adaptation continued even while invasions and rival emperors fractured his dominions.

**Sources:** [DIR: Valerian and Gallienus](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/gallval.htm).

### 64. The divided empire — Rome, Gaul, and Palmyra

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-thirty-tyrants -->
<a id="gibbon-02-thirty-tyrants"></a>

**Q:** Which regional powers divided the Roman world before Aurelian reunited it?

**A:** The **central Roman government**, a western empire founded by **Postumus in Gaul in 260**, and **Palmyra**, whose eastern power expanded under Queen Zenobia. Aurelian recovered Palmyra’s territories and the Gallic empire in **272–274**.

**More:** Gibbon rejects the label “thirty tyrants”: these rival rulers arose separately across the empire, unlike the Thirty who governed Athens together.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1229](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1229); [Gibbon, Ch. XI, L1278](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1278); [Gibbon, Ch. XI, L1300](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1300); [DIR: Postumus](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/postumus.htm); [DIR: Aurelian](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/aurelian.htm).

### 65. Usurpation — the cost paid by the provinces

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-usurpers-cost -->
<a id="gibbon-02-usurpers-cost"></a>

**Q:** Why could even a well-intentioned usurper worsen the third-century crisis?

**A:** He had to fund soldiers’ gifts, fight rival armies, and bargain with invaders. His elevation exhausted the subjects he needed to defend.

**Gibbon:** “The rapid and perpetual transitions from the cottage to the throne, and from the throne to the grave, might have amused an indifferent philosopher; were it possible for a philosopher to remain indifferent amidst the general calamities of humankind.”

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1231](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1231).

### 66. The crisis — war feeding war

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-crisis-cycle -->
<a id="gibbon-02-crisis-cycle"></a>

**Q:** How did civil war and foreign invasion reinforce each other in the third-century crisis?

**A:** Rivals diverted **troops from defence and money into military rewards**. Invasion and civil war then damaged farms and revenues, making the next army harder to support and the next frontier harder to defend.

**More:** Famine and epidemic disease compounded the losses. Political collapse was also a crisis of food, labour, and daily survival.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1231](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1231); [Gibbon, Ch. X, L1233](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1233).

### 67. Gallienus — the end

<!-- ID: gibbon-02-gallienus-death -->
<a id="gibbon-02-gallienus-death"></a>

**Q:** When and how did Gallienus die, and who followed him?

**A:** In **268**, a conspiracy of officers killed him during a supposed night alarm. The conspirators selected **Claudius II**, and the army ratified their choice.

**Sources:** [Gibbon, Ch. XI, L1254](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1254); [DIR: Valerian and Gallienus](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/gallval.htm); [Gibbon, L1254](sources/mueller-volumes-1-2.md#L1254).
