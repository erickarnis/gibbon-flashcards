# Review notes — Commodus to Gallienus

## Scope and coverage

The draft reads Chapters IV–X of the supplied Mueller abridgment in full, with the death of Gallienus taken from XI to close the sequence. It contains 66 cards and 34 literary quotations. No Anki package was made.

- IV: Commodus's inherited security, character, Danubian peace, Lucilla's conspiracy, informers, Perennis, Hercules and gladiatorial pretensions, death; Pertinax's elevation, reforms, and murder.
- V: the auction, the rival provincial armies, Julianus's fall, Severus's accession and dismissal of the guard, defeat of rivals, government, and military dependence.
- VI: Severus's disappointment and death, Geta's murder, citizenship, military finance, Caracalla's death, Macrinus, Elagabalus, Mamaea and Ulpian, Alexander's death.
- VII: succession, Maximinus, the African revolt, Gordians I–II, Pupienus/Maximus and Balbinus, Aquileia, Gordian III and Timesitheus, Philip's millennium and fall.
- VIII: Ardashir's new Persian dynasty and territorial claims, Gibbon's praise of useful religious duties, and the maxim linking armies, taxes, agriculture, and justice.
- IX: Gibbon's interpretation of poverty and liberty, warrior companions, and the immortality conferred by bards.
- X: the historian's evidence, Decius and the Goths, Gallus's peace, Aemilianus and Valerian, joint rule, the invasion fronts, Valerian's capture, Odenathus, Gallienus's literary portrait, the thirty tyrants, the cost of usurpation, and the Athens-library anecdote.

The short reigns receive compact linked narrative cards. Reign endings identify successors; transitions occasionally share one card rather than duplicate the same event. Gallienus's exit is included here; the following file should begin with Claudius.

## Chronology and names

- Commodus: retain the settled 177 co-emperorship / 180 sole accession distinction in the existing editorial notes.
- Geta: use **late 211**, not Mueller's bracketed February 212. [Michael Meckler, DIR: Geta](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/geta.htm) identifies late December 211.
- The African Gordians: use **238**, not Mueller's 237. [DIR: Gordian I](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/gordo1.htm) and [Gordian III](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/gordo3.htm) confirm the year. Avoid precise day counts and invented senatorial genealogy from the Historia Augusta.
- Alexander: his accession belongs to 222, despite the source's later bracket 223–235. The Elagabalus-death paragraph itself supplies 222.
- Pupienus is the man Gibbon calls Maximus, distinct from Maximinus Thrax. The naming equivalence is also confirmed by DIR's Gordian III biography.
- Timesitheus is Gibbon's Misitheus. His office, marriage relationship, administrative experience, and death in 243 follow DIR. Do not repeat Gibbon's description of a purely literary career without military/administrative preparation.
- Ardashir's overthrow of the Arsacids belongs to 224. The text's 211–241 bracket is not treated as the dates of the Sasanian imperial monarchy. [Encyclopaedia Iranica: Ardashir I](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ardasir-i/).
- Caracalla's citizenship edict: 212, nearly all free inhabitants; no implication that it emancipated enslaved people. [Myles Lavan, The Spread of Roman Citizenship](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/10023/12646/1/Lavan_2016_PandP_SpreadRomanCitizenship_RevisedAAM.pdf). The predominantly fiscal explanation is explicitly Gibbon's interpretation.
- Abrittus is the battlefield Gibbon calls Forum Terebronii. [DIR: Decius](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/decius.htm). No exact month is tested, and the alleged treachery of Gallus is not asserted.
- Gallienus: use the secure year 268 without Mueller's misleading March date. [DIR: Valerian and Gallienus](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/gallval.htm) describes the assassination during the siege of Aureolus at Milan.

## Uncertainty and selection

Gordian III's death is left explicitly uncertain. Gibbon follows the Roman tradition blaming Philip; Shapur's inscription claims death in battle, while DIR reviews alternatives. The card teaches the secure campaign, year, and succession without deciding between incompatible accounts. Further treatment of the competing accounts should be put to the reader if desired.

Gallienus's epigram is taught as **Gibbon's hostile portrait**, not as a balanced assessment of the emperor. The same rule governs the moral portraits of Commodus, Severus, and Maximinus. DIR's Valerian/Gallienus account offers a more substantial record of military action and explains senatorial hostility.

The Valerian mounting-block and stuffed-skin story is expressly uncertain, as Gibbon himself recognises. The supposed preservation of Athenian libraries is also presented as an anecdote he doubts; his answer about intellectual and military achievement is the literary point.

Omitted: alleged sexual scandals as established fact; Maximinus's eight-foot height and fanciful genealogy; the fabricated ancestry of the African Gordians; the disputed restoration of the censorship; a precise empire-wide mortality estimate extrapolated from Alexandria; the story of Odin as an identifiable historical legislator. These exclusions avoid turning unsteady material into factual recall. They do not prevent later, explicitly literary cards if the reader wants those passages.

The chapter VIII selections teach Gibbon's admiration for useful duties and his quotation of royal political wisdom. They do not teach his particular theology of boundless Time as a universal description of Zoroastrianism, nor turn his religious-persecution claims into independently checked fact. Chapter IX's generalisations are consistently attributed to Gibbon.

## Literary verification

Every Gibbon field was checked as an exact substring of the supplied edition, and then against the actual cited source paragraphs with `scripts/check-cards.mjs`. Excerpts may begin or end at a clause boundary; no internal omissions or altered words were introduced. Embedded sayings are introduced as sayings reported by Gibbon where needed (Pertinax, Caracalla's maxim, Ardashir). The companion-band sentence comes from Gibbon's quotation of Tacitus; it remains the supplied edition's wording, not an independently translated ancient quotation.

No existing reader decision was changed. All historical comparison commentary remains in these notes rather than the study text.
