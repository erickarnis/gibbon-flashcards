## Checked corrections and uncertainty

- **Quintillus and Aurelian (3–4):** the abridgment jumps from Claudius to Aurelian. Restore Quintillus's short intervening claim and military proclamation of Aurelian. Avoid the propagandistic claim of an unquestionable deathbed designation by Claudius. [Christian Körner, DIR: Aurelian, accession](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/aurelian.htm).
- **Aurelian's campaign order (9):** Palmyra first fell in 272; the renewed revolt was suppressed in 273; Tetricus fell in 274. Do not reproduce the source's narrative inversion placing Tetricus's submission before the campaign of 272. Same DIR source, sections 1.6–1.7. The precise January date for Aurelian's death is omitted; the year 275 is retained.
- **Tacitus (13–15):** the eight-month interregnum and purely senatorial election are not taught as fact. Modern scholarship supports military choice followed by senatorial confirmation and allows only a short interval after Aurelian's death. Gibbon's literary portrayal of a senatorial revival remains explicitly his. Tacitus's death remains uncertain between illness and murder. [Robin Mc Mahon, DIR: Tacitus](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/tacitus.htm). This correction was raised to the coordinating agent and approved as a checkable factual correction, not a request for a new reader judgment.
- **Probus (17):** Gibbon already suspects the enormous reported German casualty count; omit it rather than asking the reader to memorise an inflated number. Labour and mutiny remain, with no diagnosis or spurious exact day for the death.
- **Carus (21):** death in 283, with illness plausible and lightning an ancient report. Do not repeat the December 25 date. [William Leadbetter, DIR: Carus](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/carus.htm). That article itself contains an apparent 283-for-282 typo in its accession paragraph; its heading, context, and the companion Carinus article support 282, which is used for the transition from Probus.
- **Carinus and Numerian (22):** distinguish Caesar from Augustus. Carinus was elevated to Augustus during his father's life in 283; Numerian followed after Carus died that year. The bracket 284 must not postpone both accessions. [Leadbetter, DIR: Carinus](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/carinus.htm); [Leadbetter, DIR: Numerian](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/numerian.htm).
- **Numerian and Aper (23):** the manner of Numerian's death remains uncertain, and Diocletian's summary killing does not establish Aper's guilt. The card preserves Gibbon's irony about evidence rather than converting the accusation into a fact.
- **Carinus (24):** death during the Margus campaign in 285 is secure; murder by an officer whose wife he seduced belongs to the hostile tradition. Do not repeat the nine-wives allegation as fact. Leadbetter, DIR: Carinus, discusses both the hostile tradition and the alternative account of desertion.
- **Diocletian (34–36):** his reign ends by abdication in 305. The cabbage story and reflections on courtiers are explicitly reported anecdotes. The conflicting dates and speculative suicide account for his later death are not taught.
- **Galerius and Maximin Daia (42, 45):** retain unspecified illness or uncertainty; do not infer a modern diagnosis from religious accounts of their sufferings.
- **Constantine's final victory (46):** use 324, not the inconsistent 323 campaign bracket. His first civil war against Licinius is not assigned a date here because the source's sequence mixes 314 and 315 with chronology now often placed in 316–317. [Michael DiMaio, DIR: Licinius](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/licinius.htm).

## Religion and literary interpretation

The questions identify Gibbon when they ask for his explanatory arguments. They do not endorse his ethnic stereotypes, theological polemic, or judgments of whole religious populations. The five causes are taught as his framework, not a settled scientific model of conversion. Miracle claims remain claims; the Theophilus anecdote is studied as Gibbon's use of evidence and irony.

Gibbon's population estimate of one Christian in twenty and his estimate of fewer than two thousand judicial executions under the Great Persecution are not reliable census totals and are omitted. His closing comparison with early modern religious violence likewise depends on uncertain quantities; source criticism is captured by his powerful sentence about determining what to believe before deciding what to transcribe.

The Tetrarchy narrative does not turn the source's racialised and gendered descriptions into explanations. The Bagaudae are identified as Gibbon portrays them, without claiming that every insurgent belonged to one social class. The court-ceremony card asks about Gibbon's contrast of political styles rather than asserting that every ceremony was suddenly invented by Diocletian.

Longer quotations preserve the actual contrast or image: the theologian and historian; Diocletian's cabbages; the improbable harmony of four rulers; inherited religion and conversion; torture used for denial. Fragments were expanded where their meaning or rhythm required the full sentence. Source-comparison commentary remains here, not in the study answers.

## Persian settlement added in coverage review

Card 30 preserves the major eastern gains after Galerius defeated Narses: Mesopotamia, five trans-Tigris provinces, and Tiridates restored under Roman supremacy in Armenia. The date is **298–299** because the modern scholarly sources consulted differ: Ursula Weber, [Encyclopaedia Iranica: Narseh](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/narseh-sasanian-king/), dates the treaty to 298; A. Shapur Shahbazi, [Sasanian Dynasty](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sasanian-dynasty/?generate_pdf=1), uses 299. The supplied paragraph's bracket 297 is not reproduced as a secure exact year. The date range preserves this difference without making it a separate recall task. Gibbon's names for the river boundary and precise lists of the five provinces are omitted; modern reconstruction of the individual districts is more complex than the basic territorial outcome. The sentence about forty years of peace remains available in the source, but would merely repeat the card's consequence rather than add a strong literary passage.
