# Review notes — empire and constitution

## Coverage

The 19 companion cards fill selected institutional and literary gaps in Chapters I–III without changing the existing 40 opening cards. They cover limits to conquest; British division; legionary standards and training; colonies and citizenship; absorption of foreign cults; slavery; public buildings; roads; luxury and circulation; literary imitation; Augustus's management of the Senate, provinces, and republican powers; imperial titles; the Praetorians' choice of Claudius; and the image of universal empire as a prison.

## Editorial treatment

- Gibbon's claims about British disunity, luxury, literary decline, and constitutional liberty are presented as his arguments. These are not independent modern findings.
- The proposed slave uniform is an anecdote reported by Gibbon. The source's population estimates and its sweeping assumptions about Roman slavery are not converted into quantitative recall cards.
- Roman religious incorporation is qualified by the source's own examples of prohibition. It is not described as universal or uninterrupted toleration.
- The roads card retains the military purpose beneath the civilian benefits; the public works card distinguishes Gibbon's emphasis from a claim that every building was charitable.
- The luxury quotation ends at Gibbon's semicolon. Its continuation elaborates the landowners' incentive to improve their estates; the retained clause is a complete grammatical unit.
- The brief lictors/soldiers quotation depends on the contrast already supplied by the question and answer: senatorial governors versus imperial lieutenants. It earns its place as a pointed image of ceremonial rank and coercive power.
- The Claudius episode is included to explain the failure of republican restoration, not to start a separate comprehensive Julio-Claudian deck.
- Exact wording of all 18 quotations matches the cited passages. No omitted internal words are hidden.
